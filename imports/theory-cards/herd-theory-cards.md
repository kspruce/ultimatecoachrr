# Start here

## How to use these cards

Each card is one idea a player can read in three minutes, posted the week it is taught at training. Every card has the same nine parts: a cue as the title, why it matters, three key points, a diagram, At training, On your own, Check yourself, Next level, and Source.

- **Release:** post the card on Discord a day or two before the session in the week listed. The Release calendar lists the cards week by week, with no more than two new cards in any week. Anything about this season in particular (a 2028 priority, a squad number) sits on the Release line, so the rest of the card can be reused next year.
- **At training** says where the idea is coached. **On your own** is one thing a player can do alone before the session. **Check yourself** is one or two questions; the answers are in the card.
- **Next level** is one extra idea for experienced and elite players. Everyone else can skip it.
- **Follow-up cards** (Cards 8, 13, 16, 25, 28 and 38) collect the extra detail for experienced players and go out later in the season. They have no Next level of their own.
- **Diagram** lines say what to draw. Pull the figure from the Ultimate Coach Figures folder or sketch a new one.
- **Source** names where the card is adapted from, so you can check the detail. The codes (HN, BFD, DK and so on) are explained at the end of the Glossary.
- **Drills** (on some cards) link to the matching drills in the drill library, with their videos. The drills are what the sessions use; the card is the reading.
- **Videos** (on some cards) appear under Watch on the card’s page.
- **Glossary:** publish it in week 1 and pin it. The cards use its words without explaining them again.

## Glossary

The words these cards use, in one place. If a card uses a word you don’t know, it should be here.

**The pitch and the disc**

| Word | Means |
| --- | --- |
| Pull | The throw that starts each point. |
| Brick | A pull that lands out of bounds without being touched. The offence can start from the brick mark, in the middle of the pitch 18 m in front of its own endzone. |
| Stall | The marker’s count to ten. “Stall 5” is the count reaching five. |
| Force | The side the marker lets the thrower throw to. |
| Open side | The side of the pitch the force points to: the easier throw. |
| Break side (closed side) | The side the marker is blocking. A throw that gets there breaks the mark. |
| Trap | A force towards the nearby sideline, which leaves the thrower only a narrow strip of open side. |
| Point of attack | Where the disc is. Changing it means moving the disc across the pitch, so we attack from a new angle. |
| Upfield and downfield | Towards the endzone we are attacking. The backfield is the space behind the disc. |

**Throws**

| Word | Means |
| --- | --- |
| Huck | A long throw. |
| Dump or reset | A short pass, usually backwards, that restarts the stall count. |
| Swing | A pass across the pitch, usually between handlers. |
| Dish | A very short, soft pass to a teammate running past. |
| IO (inside-out) | A throw that starts across your body and curves back out towards your throwing side. |
| OI (outside-in) | A throw that starts wide on your throwing side and curves back in. |
| Around | A break throw that goes wide, round the marker. |
| Hammer, scoober, blade | Throws that go over defenders. The hammer and scoober fly upside down; a blade is thrown on a steep edge. |
| Point block | The marker blocking the throw as it is released. |

**Offence**

| Word | Means |
| --- | --- |
| Handler | A player who sets up near the disc and does most of the throwing. |
| Cutter | A player who sets up downfield and runs to get open. |
| 3H | A third handler. |
| Under | A cut back towards the disc. |
| Away or deep | A cut away from the disc, towards the endzone we are attacking. |
| Upline | A handler’s cut from behind or level with the disc, running upfield past the thrower. |
| Power position | Catching the disc while you are moving upfield with your marker trailing, so you can throw before the mark is set. |
| Clear | Leaving a space after your cut so that a teammate can use it. |
| Continuation | The next cut after a completed pass, timed so the disc keeps moving. |
| Give-and-go | Throw, run, and get the disc straight back. Card 26. |
| Vert | The vertical stack: cutters line up down the middle of the pitch. Card 35. |
| Horo or ho | The horizontal stack: cutters spread across the pitch. Card 36. |
| Dominator | Two or three handlers working the disc upfield with give-and-goes. Card 36. |

**Defence**

| Word | Means |
| --- | --- |
| Mark or marker | The defender on the thrower. |
| Person D | Each defender guards one player. |
| Zone | Defenders guard areas of the pitch, not players. Cards 40 and 41. |
| Cup | In a zone, the defenders around the thrower who block the forward throws. |
| Buffer | The gap a defender keeps between themselves and their cutter. |
| Poach | Leaving your player to defend a space or a throw. Card 33. |
| Clog | Standing in a throwing lane to block it. |
| Switch | Two defenders swapping players. Card 34. |

**Games and the club**

| Word | Means |
| --- | --- |
| 1s and 2s | Herd’s first and second teams. |
| Joint and split sessions | In a joint session both squads train together. In a split session the 1s and 2s train separately. |
| C1 to C12 | The classroom sessions. |
| O-line and D-line | The players who start points on offence, and those who start on defence. |
| Hold and break | A hold is a goal by the team that started the point on offence. A break is a goal by the team that started on defence. (A break throw is something else: see Break side.) |
| Universe point | The deciding point, when the next goal wins. |
| Pick | A violation: an offensive player gets in the way of a defender who is following their own player. |

## Card 1 · The Herd Way: five habits, three jobs, one language

**Release:** week 1. 2028 season: 44 players across two squads, one language.

**Why it matters.** Herd is a place where everyone gets better, from ten-year veterans to players a year in. We get there faster when everyone shares the same habits and the same words.

**Three key points**

1. **The Five Frisbee Habits.** Sprint your cuts. Cut up and down, not across. Check behind you when you go deep. Look for the dump by stall 5. Only throw to players who are free.
2. **Every touch has a job.** It should gain yards, change the point of attack, or break the mark. If it does none of these, ask why you threw it.
3. **Say it out loud.** Call the move as you make it:

| Call | Means |
| --- | --- |
| Dump | Any pass behind the disc |
| Mac | Upline cut from level with or above the disc |
| Zip | Vertical cut for a short throw on the move |
| Dynamic dump | Dump caught on the closed side that flows straight to the break side |
| Negative / positive | Reset set behind the disc (about 45°) on the open side / ahead (about 30°) on the break side |

**Coaching cue:** “It is never a bad idea to use the dump.”

**Diagram:** a disc in the middle of the pitch with the negative reset (open side, behind) and positive reset (break side, ahead) marked.

**At training:** week 1 split session. Walk through the vocabulary, then 2v2 calling each move. The 2s run Sprint Relay and Triple Cut for the Five Habits.

**On your own:** learn the five calls. Then watch ten minutes of any ultimate game and say the call out loud each time you spot one.

**Check yourself:** What are the three jobs a touch can have? By which stall should you be looking for the dump?

**Next level:** place yourself on the rookie → substitute → starter → baller ladder for throwing, cutting and defence separately. Pick the one where you are lowest and set a process goal you can hit several times a session.

**Source:** DK’s Bible 6.11; BFD 1.2; HN Amsterdam Conference (From Rookie to Baller, Process over Outcome).

## Card 2 · Rules and Spirit in 10 minutes

**Release:** week 1

**Why it matters.** Ultimate is self-refereed, so every player is also an official. Knowing the common calls keeps games fast and fair, and saves arguments at tournaments.

**Three key points**

1. **The stall count.** The marker says “Stalling” and counts one to ten, at least a second apart, from within 3 m of the pivot. Not released before “ten” is a turnover. A second defender within 3 m of the thrower who isn’t guarding anyone else is a double team.
2. **Calls stop play; talk resolves them.** A foul or violation stops play. The player called on can accept or “Contest”. The players involved and those with the best view discuss it. If you can’t agree, the disc goes back to the last undisputed thrower. Restart with a check.
3. **Spirit is a skill we practise.** Aim to give every opponent the best-spirited game they have had. Make only the calls that matter. Keep discussions under 45 seconds.

**Coaching cue:** “Compete hard, call fair, talk short.”

**Diagram:** none. Link the [WFDF pocket rules](https://rules.wfdf.sport/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/WFDF-Rules-of-Ultimate-2025-2028-Pocket-Format.pdf) and a one-page hand-signal sheet.

**At training:** in week 1 free games, the sideline calls “freeze” on one disputed call per game and the two players talk it through out loud.

**On your own:** read the WFDF pocket rules linked above (about ten minutes). Note one rule you didn’t know and bring it to training.

**Check yourself:** What happens if the two players can’t agree on a call? How long should a discussion take at most?

**Next level:** know the stall restarts. After an accepted defensive call, restart at 1. After an accepted offensive call, restart at a maximum of 9. After a contested stall-out, restart at 8. After everything else, including a pick, restart at a maximum of 6.

**Source:** [WFDF Rules of Ultimate 2025–2028](https://rules.wfdf.sport/resources/) (rules 1.10–1.12, 9, 15–16, 18.1); HN Coach Corner, Practicing Spirit.

## Card 3 · How we learn: the Heckler and the Hero

**Release:** week 2, as the pre-read for classroom session C1 in week 3

**Why it matters.** Most players learn more when they think less in the moment. Knowing which voice is talking helps you train hard, play freely and recover from mistakes fast.

**Three key points**

1. **Two selves.** The Heckler instructs, judges and doubts. The Hero is your trained body and instincts. Let the Heckler plan and review; in games, trust the Hero.
2. **Notice, don’t judge.** Ask yourself what you saw: “Where did that release finish?” “What did I see before I cut?” Awareness fixes more than criticism does.
3. **Reset in one breath.** After a mistake, take one breath and say one cue word, then go straight to your next job.

**Coaching cue:** pick your own cue word, such as “soft hands” or “patient and sharp”.

**Diagram:** two columns. Heckler: plans, reviews, judges, worries. Hero: reacts, flows, trusts, stays present.

**At training:** in week 3 catching drills, partners swap “don’t do that” for one question: “What did you notice?”

**On your own:** choose your cue word tonight. In your next throwing session, after every miss take one breath, say it, and throw again.

**Check yourself:** Which self should plan and review, and which should play? What do you do in the breath after a mistake?

**Next level:** train in your zone of proximal development. If every rep goes perfectly, make it harder; if every rep fails, make it easier. Mistakes on some reps mean you’re learning.

**Source:** The Inner Game of Ultimate Frisbee (Adams); HN Coach Corner (Getting out of your head; Zone of Proximal Development).

# Throw and catch

## Card 4 · Grip, pivot and fakes: be a threat on every catch

**Release:** week 2. 2028 priority: breaking the mark, a weak point last season.

**Why it matters.** Breaking the mark starts here. A balanced thrower with a big pivot and believable fakes forces the marker to guess.

**Three key points**

1. **Balance first.** Step into the throw with your weight on the non-pivot foot and stay upright. You shouldn’t need an extra step afterwards. Start every pivot from home position: both hands on the disc, lined up with your centre.
2. **Use your 360 degrees.** Step close to the marker for quick, flat throws and further away for wider ones. Pivot directly left or right, never straight forward or back.
3. **One real fake beats three lazy ones.** A fake must look like the throw: full arm, eyes and shoulders, the same release point. Fake one side, then throw the other.

**Coaching cue:** “Step, flick, point.” Finish with your arm pointing at the target.

**Diagram:** a thrower and marker from above, showing the pivot’s reach to each side and the break-side space behind the marker.

**At training:** week 2 joint session, compass throwing and fake-fake-throw. The split sessions use the 3-handler game.

**On your own:** 20 pivots in front of a mirror or window: home position, step, fake, back to home. No extra step, and every fake should look like a throw.

**Check yourself:** Where is your weight when you step into a throw? What makes a fake believable?

**Next level:** learn the shimmy. Fake the first six inches of a pivot, rock your shoulders and keep a throwable grip. If the marker doesn’t bite, finish the pivot and throw. Card 8 adds a counter to your best move.

**Source:** DK’s Bible 4.3–4.8; BFD ch. 1; HN Point of Attack (The Shimmy); HN Amsterdam Conference (Breaking a Defensive Heart).

## Card 5 · Catching: feet first, then hands

**Release:** week 3

**Why it matters.** Every drop is a turnover we gave away for free. Most drops come from the feet, not the hands: if your body isn’t behind the disc, your hands are already in trouble.

**Three key points**

1. **Feet first.** Move your body behind the disc, splay your fingers, clap the centre or leading edge, then bring it in.
2. **Two hands whenever you can.** Use the pancake (clap) at chest height and the crab claw above or below. Save one-handed catches for when you have no choice.
3. **Read the disc three times.** Read it off the hand: nose up means slow and floaty, flat means fast. Watch the edge in flight. At the catch, find your high point and don’t get trapped underneath it.

**Coaching cue:** “Never say sorry before the disc hits the ground.”

**Diagram:** pancake catch at the chest and crab claw high and low, with the spinning edge marked.

**At training:** week 3 joint session, the heads-on drill and then the square drill, followed by the Multi-disc game.

**On your own:** 100 catches against a wall or with a friend: 50 pancake, 25 crab claw high, 25 crab claw low. Move your feet behind every one.

**Check yourself:** Where do most drops start? When do you use the crab claw instead of the pancake?

**Drills:** [Turn and attack (GRUT)](/sessions/drills?q=Turn+and+attack) · [Rinkydink (GRUT)](/sessions/drills?q=Rinkydink)

**Next level:** attack the disc. Take a hard step as you catch and run through it in go-to drills. If you never drop in drills, you aren’t attacking hard enough. Catching in the air is on Card 20.

**Source:** UAP Catching (Shardlow); HB 15.1–15.2; HN Excel Premium (Reading the Disc); HN Next Gen 10 (Winning Discs in the Air); HN Handler Offense (Moment of Catching).

## Card 6 · Throw to space, not to people

**Release:** week 5

**Why it matters.** Retention starts here. A receiver running into space is easier to hit and harder to defend than a receiver standing still.

**Three key points**

1. **Lead the receiver.** Throw into the space they are running towards, not where they are now. Aim for the top of the chest when they arrive.
2. **Throw on their turn, not their arrival.** Release as the cutter plants and turns. Cutters, start before the thrower catches so you’re open as they look.
3. **Make it easy to catch.** A disc that fades towards the receiver slows down and arrives at catchable height. A disc fading away speeds up and is much harder to catch.

**Coaching cue:** “Space, not face.”

**Diagram:** a cutter’s path with the disc landing ahead of them, marked “throw here”, and a ghost receiver at the release point marked “not here”.

**At training:** week 5 joint session, the lead-pass drill, then Space Hopper and Golden Hand.

**On your own:** 30 throws at a tree or goalpost from 15 m. Drive the disc through the target with a full follow-through rather than guiding it: accuracy comes from spin.

**Check yourself:** When do you release: as the cutter arrives, or as they turn? Why is a disc fading towards the receiver easier to catch?

**Drills:** [Under-to-away lead passes (GRUT)](/sessions/drills?q=Under-to-away) · [20-20-20 (GRUT)](/sessions/drills?q=20-20-20)

**Next level:** throw to the shoulder away from the defender, and a little wider than expected, so the catch flows straight into the next throw.

**Source:** DK’s Bible 9.12; Flik L1 and L4; BFD (timing); HN Flik (Ideal Reception); HN Excel Premium (Accuracy).

## Card 7 · Breaking the mark: wide, low, decisive

**Release:** week 10; re-shared in weeks 15 and 32

**Why it matters.** A team that can break the mark can attack the whole pitch. If we can’t, the defence only has to guard half of it.

**Three key points**

1. **Width and height beat the mark.** Release wide of the marker’s body or low, below the knee. Hold your finish for two seconds to prove your balance.
2. **Choose early IO or late around.** The break-side space is only behind the marker. Throw an inside-out (IO) break early in the stall, or fake into a late around, and commit to it.
3. **Break with a purpose.** Every touch should gain yards, change the point of attack or break the mark. A break should lead to a throw that gains yards next.

**Coaching cue:** “Wide, low, hold it.”

**Diagram:** a thrower and triangle mark from above, with the IO break at about 75° into the break side and the around break past the marker’s foot.

**At training:** week 10 joint session, wide-release pairs, the cone-width ladder, low releases, then Get 10 with no throws above the marker’s arm. Finish with Classic Breakmark, the Herd classic.

**On your own:** the 10-minute low-release session (linked under Drills), or 20 wide and 20 low releases on each side, holding every finish for two seconds.

**Check yourself:** Where is the break-side space? When in the stall do you throw the IO, and when the around?

**Drills:** [10-minute low-release session (GRUT)](/sessions/drills?q=low-release+session) · [Breakside flow (GRUT)](/sessions/drills?q=Breakside+flow)

**Next level:** train against an extended mark. Getting point-blocked sometimes means you’re finding your limits. Card 8 adds quick releases and counters.

**Source:** BFD ch. 1; DK (Breaking the mark); UAP Breaking the Mark (Saul); HN Practice Sessions 5–6; HN 10 Big Drills (Breakmark); HN Next Gen 10.

## Card 8 · Breaking the mark 2: counters and quick releases

**Release:** week 32, with the breaking refresher. Follow-up to Cards 4, 7 and 23.

**Why it matters.** By now, markers know your favourite break and sit on it. You don’t need ten throws to beat them. You need a counter they have to respect, and a release that has gone before they can react.

**Three key points**

1. **One great move plus a counter.** Build one move you trust, then the counter to it, so the marker has to respect both. Never throw the same throw twice in a row.
2. **Release before they’re set.** The quick-release backhand goes before your pivot foot lands, just to get it past the marker’s shoulder. The half-pivot throw goes before your non-pivot foot lands.
3. **Add range, then keep moving.** Pick one or two unusual throws to master. After a break, watch for the inverse give-and-go when the marker overcommits.

**Coaching cue:** “Move, counter, gone.”

**Diagram:** a thrower and marker from above, showing one move and its counter on the opposite side of the marker.

**At training:** week 32 joint session, the breaking refresher against an extended mark, then the Break-Throw Bonus Point game.

**On your own:** name your best break and its counter. Throw 20 of each, alternating, so you never throw the same one twice in a row.

**Check yourself:** Why do you need a counter? When does the quick-release backhand leave your hand?

**Source:** collected from the Next level notes of Cards 4, 7, 23 and 26. HN Amsterdam Conference (Breaking a Defensive Heart); UAP Breaking the Mark (Saul); HN Next Gen 10; HN Handler Offense (Give-Go Moves).

## Card 9 · The huck: control, not power

**Release:** week 28. Huck flight is introduced earlier, in week 5; technique is on Cards 10 and 11.

**Why it matters.** One good huck scores in a single throw. One bad huck hands the disc back with the full pitch to defend. Good hucks are about control, not throwing as hard as you can.

**Three key points**

1. **Set it up first.** Huck from power position (you’ve caught the disc moving upfield with your marker trailing) or straight after a swing, before the mark sets.
2. **Shape it.** Inside-out (IO) early, flat through the middle, outside-in (OI) at the end, so it floats into space. Lead it slightly to the break side, over the defender’s shoulder.
3. **Build it like any throw.** Use an extra-tight grip, weight on the non-pivot foot, arm close to the body and a full wrist flick. Finish pointing at the target. In games, wind up to about 75% of your max range.

**Coaching cue:** “Overthrown beats underthrown.”

**Diagram:** the IO → flat → OI flight path, landing on the break side ahead of the cutter.

**At training:** week 28 joint session, hucking shapes and deep-space games. Score with Archery: +2 caught in stride, +1 overthrown, 0 if the cutter has to wait.

**On your own:** the hucking alley: 10 hucks into a 20 m lane, counting how many land in. Then throw a few at 110% of your range.

**Check yourself:** When is the right moment to huck? Which is the better miss, overthrown or underthrown?

**Drills:** [Read-the-defender hucking (GRUT)](/sessions/drills?q=Read-the-defender). Technique: Cards 10 and 11.

**Next level:** huck past a mark. Pump-fake one side and huck the other. Against a flat, no-huck mark, add OI and aim outside the receiver.

**Source:** DK’s Bible 4.7; Flik L4 Lesson 4; HN Practice Sessions 9; HN Excel Premium (Improve your Hucking, Hucking with a Mark, Backhand Huck); HN Deep Dives (Ring of Fire Hucking).

## Card 10 · The backhand huck: step sideways, pull it through

**Release:** week 12, as reading for the Christmas break; re-shared in week 28, when it is taught

**Why it matters.** The backhand huck is the long throw most players can learn to control. Power comes from the step and the turn of the body, not from the arm, so a small thrower with good technique can out-huck a big one.

**Three key points**

1. **Step sideways.** Unlike the forehand, step to the side, not forward. A comfortable length: too big and you lose balance, too small and you lose power.
2. **Load, then pull.** Wind up until your shoulders face away from the target, then rotate explosively towards it. The disc is pulled through by the turn, not pushed by the arm.
3. **Release long and slightly IO.** Release further forward than a normal backhand, with your whole arm stretched out in front: the longer the contact, the more power. A slight inside-out angle flattens out in the air.

**Coaching cue:** “Turn away, pull it through.”

**Diagram:** a thrower from above: sideways step, shoulders turned away at the load, then the arm fully extended towards the target at release.

**At training:** week 28 joint session, backhand huck technique, building up from wrist only to step to full throw. Check progress on the hucking alley benchmark.

**On your own:** over the break, 20 backhand hucks with a partner or into an empty field: five wrist only, five adding the step, ten full. Film one from the side and check that your shoulders turn away.

**Check yourself:** Where does the power come from? Which way do you step?

**Videos:** [Backhand huck technique (GRUT)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HwuTNpxnzKo)

**Next level:** add a pump fake before the step, so the mark has to respect the short throw too. Card 9 covers when to huck and how to shape it.

**Source:** GRUT (Amsterdam) huck video, filmed with Flik Ultimate; UAP Backhand hucks.

## Card 11 · The forehand huck: load it, snap it

**Release:** week 13, as reading for the Christmas break; re-shared in week 28, when it is taught, and in week 30 for hucking downwind

**Why it matters.** A forehand huck gets off quickly and on the break side, so it beats marks that take away the backhand. Like the backhand, its power comes from loading the body, not from throwing harder.

**Three key points**

1. **Load sideways.** Stretch the arm back like an elastic band: the more it stretches, the more energy it stores. As your front foot lands, the arm is pulled forward, not pushed.
2. **Turn the shoulders.** They face the field at the start, turn side-on as you wind up, and turn back to the field as you release.
3. **Snap and plant.** Pull the arm back straight after the release for a snappy release and lots of spin, which helps most downwind. Planting your foot hard just before you throw adds a little extra speed.

**Coaching cue:** “Stretch, turn, snap.”

**Diagram:** a thrower side-on at full stretch, an arrow showing the shoulder turn back to the field, and the arm snapping back after release.

**At training:** week 28 joint session, forehand huck technique, then deep-space games. In week 30 use the snap for downwind hucks.

**On your own:** 20 forehand hucks, snapping the arm back after each release. Then throw five backhand and five forehand hucks at the same target, so a mark can’t take one away.

**Check yourself:** What does pulling the arm back after the release add? How do your shoulders move through the throw?

**Videos:** [Forehand huck technique (GRUT)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=utwBy0UtL60)

**Next level:** combine all three loads (sideways, rotational and the foot plant) and practise at 110% of your range.

**Source:** GRUT (Amsterdam) huck video, filmed with Flik Ultimate.

## Card 12 · Throwing in the wind

**Release:** week 26, before a usually windy Tour 1; re-shared in week 30

**Why it matters.** UK tournaments are often windy. In wind, every extra throw is another chance to turn it over, so the team that adapts its throws and its plan wins the breaks.

**Three key points**

1. **Low upwind, high downwind, more spin.** Upwind, keep it low and flat. Downwind, release high over the mark; an OI edge slows a huck down.
2. **Bigger spaces, fewer throws.** In gusty wind, play in big spaces and slow the tempo. Huck downwind to an isolated cutter after a swing. Make big under cuts upwind.
3. **Back each other up.** Get bodies under every disc, and get the disc off the downwind sideline.

**Coaching cue:** “Low into it, high with it.”

**Diagram:** a pitch with a wind arrow, labelling the upwind “high side” and the downwind “low side”, with a low release going upwind and a high release going downwind.

**At training:** week 25 joint session, pulls in wind. Week 30 joint session, throwing in all directions and upwind–downwind games.

**On your own:** on the next windy day, throw 20 upwind and 20 downwind with a partner. Add spin until the disc stops wobbling.

**Check yourself:** Which way do you release low, and which way high? Which sideline do you get the disc off?

**Drills:** [10-minute low-release session (GRUT)](/sessions/drills?q=low-release+session)

**Next level:** in a crosswind, swings towards the high side are harder but worth more. Card 13 turns this into a plan for the whole team.

**Source:** DK’s Bible 4.9; Flik Zone Offence 8; HN Deep Dives (Extreme Wind: Molly Brown v Phoenix; Firetails Downwind Offence).

## Card 13 · The wind plan: decide before the first pull

**Release:** week 30. Follow-up to Card 12.

**Why it matters.** Wind changes which end you want, how the offence sets up and which defence to call. A team that decides those before the game spends its points playing; a team that decides mid-point gives away breaks.

**Three key points**

1. **Decide the toss before the game.** Know which end and which start you want. In strong wind, choosing defence and pulling downwind can earn an early break.
2. **Downwind, sit deeper.** Cutters can set up deeper, which opens big, flat under cuts. Huck to an isolated cutter after a swing, with an OI edge to slow the disc down.
3. **Pick the defence by the wind.** The Bull zone (Card 40) turns every extra throw into a chance for a turnover. In a strong crosswind, the arrowhead (Card 41) pushes the disc to the sideline. Listen for the wind call.

**Coaching cue:** “Plan it before the pull.”

**Diagram:** a pitch with a wind arrow, showing the end we choose at the toss and the downwind offence with its cutters set deeper.

**At training:** week 30 joint session, upwind–downwind 7v7. In the split sessions the 1s build the wind plan around the arrowhead and line trap, and the 2s play a safe vert stack in wind.

**On your own:** before your next windy game, write down which end you would choose at the toss, and why. Compare it with what the captains chose.

**Check yourself:** In strong wind, why might we choose to start on defence? Where do cutters set up when we’re going downwind?

**Source:** collected from the Next level notes of Card 12. DK’s Bible 4.9; HN Deep Dives (Extreme Wind: Molly Brown v Phoenix; Firetails Downwind Offence).

# Get open

## Card 14 · The first three steps: accelerate, stop, change direction

**Release:** week 4; re-shared in week 18 for the benchmark. 2028 priority: cutting under pressure, a weak point last season.

**Why it matters.** Cutting under pressure is mostly won in the first few steps. Without a track or gym, this footwork is our speed work.

**Three key points**

1. **Lean, then lots of small steps.** Lean until you start to fall, then drive with short, fast steps. Take the first five steps with your head down.
2. **Chop, don’t plant.** To change direction, chop your feet, stay low for a moment, then push off your outside foot. A well-trained chop step is faster than a hard plant and easier on your knees.
3. **Stay low through every turn.** Get into an athletic, hockey-stop position, with your hips and chest down, before you change direction.

**Coaching cue:** “Low, chop, explode.”

**Diagram:** the 5-10-5 shuttle and a Hollywood square (Z, C and X patterns on a 7 m square), with the chop points marked.

**At training:** week 4 joint session, 5-10-5 and hockey stops, then ladders and Hollywood squares. Keep the volume low and rest fully: this is speed, not fitness. Your 5-10-5 is filmed in week 1 and again at the February benchmark (week 18), so you can compare the two.

**On your own:** three times this week: five 10 m starts from a lean, then five chop stops on each foot. Walk back between reps.

**Check yourself:** Why chop instead of plant? Where is your head for the first five steps?

**Next level:** add the jump cut: a small hop straight up and down, sell a shoulder fake as you land, then explode. Card 16 adds the jab and the hesitation cut.

**Source:** HB Tr 14; UAP Changing Direction (Witmer); HN Practice Sessions 1+2; HN 10 Big Drills; HN Excel Premium (Cutting Class); HN Deep Dives (Italy Mixed WUC 2024).

## Card 15 · Getting open: make the defender commit

**Release:** week 4, alongside Card 14

**Why it matters.** It’s the cutter’s job to get free for the thrower. There are few bad throws, but many bad options. Getting open is about beating your defender’s position, not just outrunning them.

**Three key points**

1. **Commit to the cut.** Two or three steps fake the thrower; five or six steps fake the defender. A half-cut is worse than no cut.
2. **Get their hips turned.** Drive hard one way until the defender commits their hips, then attack the space behind them. Charge them first and they’ll step back and overcommit.
3. **You choose when the race starts.** Start when you’re close to your defender and already moving. Set up from a jog so you can watch where they’re overcommitting, then be decisive.

**Coaching cue:** “Jog, jab, sprint.”

**Diagram:** three cut shapes side by side: away-then-under, under-then-away, and the double move (away, fake under, away).

**At training:** week 4 split session, the five-cone drill for the three cut families. Then in week 5, 1v1 “name your cut”, where the cutter calls the cut first and still has to win.

**On your own:** watch one point of any game and follow a single cutter. Count the steps they take before each change of direction: was it enough to turn the defender’s hips?

**Check yourself:** How many steps does it take to fake a defender? When do you start the race?

**Next level:** against faster or stronger defenders, win the race to a shoulder and seal the lane with your body. Afterwards, ask your defender when they were off balance. Card 16 has more ways to get open.

**Source:** UAP Cutting (Raynor); UAP Handler O (Wiggins); HB App. B; Flik L4 Lesson 3; HN Excel Premium (Isolation Cutting: Jab Step, Out and Under, Double Move, Seal Cut, Setting up your Cut).

## Card 16 · More ways to get open: jab, hesitate, vary

**Release:** week 35. Follow-up to Cards 14 and 15.

**Why it matters.** By mid-season, defenders have seen your favourite cut. A second and a third way to get open keeps them guessing, and none of them needs more speed.

**Three key points**

1. **One jab can be enough.** Italy’s mixed team got open at WUC 2024 with a single quick jab rather than big cuts. Close the buffer before you jab.
2. **The hesitation cut.** Lift the inside leg and turn your shoulders as if going under, then go deep.
3. **Have five ways.** Rush, jab, drive, wait and seal. Know which two are yours now, and pick one to add.

**Coaching cue:** “One jab, then go.”

**Diagram:** a cutter and defender with the buffer closed, a single jab step, then the sprint the other way; beside it, the hesitation cut.

**At training:** week 35, the 1s v 2s scrimmage. Use a different way to get open on each point you play, and ask your defender afterwards which one worked. Refreshed in week 38’s cutting session.

**On your own:** film ten jab steps and ten hesitation cuts against a cone. Does the fake look like the real cut for its first step?

**Check yourself:** What do you do before you jab? Name the five ways to get open.

**Source:** collected from the Next level notes of Cards 14 and 15. HN Excel Premium (Cutting Class; Isolation Cutting: Jab Step); HN Deep Dives (Italy Mixed WUC 2024).

## Card 17 · Clearing is cutting: make space before you take it

**Release:** week 6

**Why it matters.** Most of the time you don’t have the disc and aren’t the cutter, so most of your value comes from the space you make. In the words of the Herd notebook: 25% attacking space, 75% clearing it.

**Three key points**

1. **Your clear is a cut.** Run it hard and with purpose. A lazy clear blocks the next cutter and lets your defender poach.
2. **Know the three spaces.** Active space is where we’re attacking, inactive space is where we’re not, and ready space is where we wait to set up. In a vert stack, the open side is active, the break side inactive and the stack is ready space. They change every time the disc moves.
3. **Never more than two in a space.** If a teammate is already cutting there, the best cut is often letting them have it. Take the space their cut creates instead.

**Coaching cue:** “Make it, then take it.”

**Diagram:** a vert stack showing the active, inactive and ready spaces, a clearing cutter’s path out of the lane, and the next cutter attacking the space they left.

**At training:** week 6 joint session, Honeypass, then the clear-cut drill and the gate drill, then Caterpillar.

**On your own:** watch five minutes of any game and follow one player who doesn’t have the disc. Count their clears and their cuts.

**Check yourself:** What are the three spaces, and which is which in a vert stack? What is the most players we want in one space?

**Next level:** avoid the five cutting mistakes: not committing, clearing too early, clearing slowly, too many cuts in one space, and ignoring the break side. The horo stack has its own shapes: see Card 36.

**Source:** UAP Cutting (Raynor, after Aguilera); HB App. B; BFD (field roles); HN Excel Premium (Common Mistakes, Principles); HN Flik (Cutting, Vertical Stack, Horizontal Stack).

## Card 18 · Reading the pitch: read, process, move

**Release:** week 17. 2028 priority: reading the pitch, a weak point last season.

**Why it matters.** The best players don’t see more than you; they look earlier and know what to look for.

**Three key points**

1. **Read, process, move.** Read: where’s the force, the disc and the thrower? Process: what does that mean for speed, angle and the defender? Move: make the decision. Do the reading before you catch.
2. **Close isn’t covered.** Don’t only look for distance. Check the cutter’s and the defender’s hips and eyes. A cutter with no separation whose hips have turned the defender is open if you lead them into space.
3. **Know where the danger is.** The most dangerous space changes with the disc’s position. On defence, take away the dangerous option first, then help.

**Coaching cue:** “Look before you catch.”

**Diagram:** two snapshots of the same cutter and defender. In one they’re close and covered; in the other they’re close but open, because the defender’s hips face the wrong way.

**At training:** week 17 joint session, the asymmetric offence game. At water breaks, show a game screenshot for two seconds and ask: what’s the force, who’s open, would you huck?

**On your own:** pause any game film just before five different throws. Each time, name the force, who’s open and what you’d throw, then press play.

**Check yourself:** What are the three steps, and when do you do the first? Why can a cutter with no separation still be open?

**Next level:** read the game in time. An attacking flow usually lasts about 20 seconds and involves three or four attackers, so the other defenders have time to help. Card 33 covers when to leave your player.

**Source:** HN Excel Premium (Game IQ: the RMP technique; Fixing Focus: Close vs Covered); BFD (Defensive fundamentals); HN Flik (When to Poach, Flashing the Lane).

## Card 19 · Deep cutting: go when the door opens

**Release:** week 28, alongside Card 9

**Why it matters.** Deep cutting is about opportunity, not running deep all the time. Cut too early and the thrower sees you 20 m away, so the huck has to go even further.

**Three key points**

1. **Wait for the trigger.** Go deep when the thrower is in power position, after an upline cut, or as the continuation after a swing. Start shallow so the space stays open.
2. **Cut through the middle, not the sideline.** Cutting along the sideline narrows your angle and gives the defender a window on the throw.
3. **Sprint, then look sideways.** Take the first five steps with your head down to accelerate. Then glance sideways at the disc rather than turning back to the thrower.

**Coaching cue:** “Shallow, wait, go.”

**Diagram:** the L cut: a lateral cut to the break side at 60–70% pace, a plant, then a vertical strike deep, with the huck landing in stride.

**At training:** week 28 joint session, deep-space games. Practise deep-cut timing with the Dishy Huck drill.

**On your own:** watch three hucks in any game. Where was the cutter when the thrower caught the disc? Did they start shallow?

**Check yourself:** What are the three triggers for a deep cut? Why cut through the middle rather than along the sideline?

**Drills:** [Read-the-defender hucking (GRUT)](/sessions/drills?q=Read-the-defender). Winning the contest: Card 20.

**Next level:** run the L cut. Start it once the swing is caught, come far enough across to break the thrower’s plane, let the defender bite, then strike deep. If the defender takes away the deep, counter back to the open side.

**Source:** HN Excel Premium (Deep Cutting, The L Cut, How to Jump); HN Next Gen 10 (Deep Cut Timing); HN 10 Big Drills; HB 27.3–27.4.

## Card 20 · Winning the deep ball: box out, find the body

**Release:** week 25, alongside aerial contests and deep defence

**Why it matters.** Many deep throws end in a contest. The player who wins the position, not the one who jumps highest, usually comes down with the disc.

**Three key points**

1. **On offence, box out.** When the deep throw goes up, keep your body between the disc and your defender. Read the disc early and take the space in front of them.
2. **Change your run if you need to.** Slow down, push out a little, or curve your cut, so the defender can’t get their body between you and the disc.
3. **On defence, find the body first.** Don’t run to where you think the disc will land. Run to the cutter, touch them and get as close as possible, then jump for the block when the disc arrives.

**Coaching cue:** “Offence: body between. Defence: body first.”

**Diagram:** a deep throw with the cutter boxing out in front of the defender; beside it, the defender’s path straight to the cutter’s body, not to the disc.

**At training:** week 25 joint session, the 1v1 deep drill: three rounds (nobody jumps, offence jumps, both jump), then swap roles so everyone practises both halves.

**On your own:** throw a disc high to yourself 20 times and catch each one at the top of your jump. Add a friend as a defender when you can.

**Check yourself:** On offence, where should your body be when the disc goes up? On defence, where do you run first?

**Videos:** [Deep box-out (GRUT)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2mc9OVdpwMs) · [Find the body (GRUT)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GAXfWzZi7c8)

**See also:** Card 19 for the deep cut; Card 31 for staying goal-side, so the cutter never gets free behind you.

**Next level:** win it in the air. Read the throw, hold your position, then convert your speed into height: reach your leg forward, plant your heel, pop up and go up with your outside hand. If the disc hangs, attack it at its highest point.

**Source:** GRUT (Amsterdam) deep drill videos, filmed with Flik Ultimate; HN Practice Sessions 10; HB 27.4–27.5.

# Handle and reset

## Card 21 · The handler’s job: forward, then dump

**Release:** week 3; re-shared in week 14 for resets under a live mark

**Why it matters.** Handlers keep the disc alive and move it to where the cutters can attack. Most turnovers at high stall come from a handler who looked upfield for too long, or a reset that arrived too late.

**Three key points**

1. **Look forward once, then dump.** On the catch, throw forward or make a convincing forward fake, then engage the dump by stall 4–5.
2. **Be open when the thrower turns.** Time your handler cut so you’re free the moment the thrower looks at you, not before or after.
3. **On the sideline, getting off it is the only priority.** Everything else can wait.

**Coaching cue:** “Forward, then dump by five.”

**Diagram:** a thrower with a forward look (arrow upfield, marked stall 1–3) and then the dump behind (marked stall 4–5).

**At training:** week 3 split session, look-forward-once-then-dump (stall 5 for the 1s, stall 7 for the 2s). Every player handles in 2s games.

**On your own:** in your next game of any kind, count the stall in your head on every catch. Did you turn to the dump by five?

**Check yourself:** What do you do first on the catch? By which stall do you engage the dump?

**Next level:** keep the field big. Set up dumps at least 10 m from the thrower and offset about 20°, not flat across the pitch, so each dump carries two threats.

**Source:** Hildo Bijl (HB) Tr 13, 26.2–26.3; DK’s Bible 6.11; HN Handler Offense.

## Card 22 · Resets: negative on the open side, positive on the break side

**Release:** week 14. 2028 priority: retention. Resets were the base of that work last season.

**Why it matters.** A good reset doesn’t just lower the stall count; it gives the offence a new angle to attack from.

**Three key points**

1. **Set up by side.** On the open side, set negative: behind the disc at about 45°. On the break side, set positive: ahead of the disc at about 30°.
2. **Two threats.** A dump on the closed (break) side threatens across the field and upfield. A dump on the open side threatens a short pass backwards and upfield. Always keep at least two.
3. **Dump and swing.** The thrower squares up to the reset. The reset steps upfield to commit their defender, then comes back into the backfield away from the sideline. Swing cutters go late, not early.

**Coaching cue:** “It is never a bad idea to use the dump.”

**Diagram:** two thrower positions, one with the reset negative on the open side and one with it positive on the break side, plus the swing path after the dump.

**At training:** week 14 split session, resets under a live mark. The idea is first met in week 1: 2v2 on a long, narrow pitch where the only legal throw before stall 5 is an assist.

**On your own:** draw a pitch three times, with the disc on each sideline and in the middle. Mark where the reset sets up each time: negative on the open side, positive on the break side.

**Check yourself:** At what angle does the reset set on the open side, and on the break side? Do swing cutters go early or late?

**Drills:** [Breakside flow (GRUT)](/sessions/drills?q=Breakside+flow)

**Next level:** every throw that loses yards must be followed by a change in the point of attack (BFD’s Jedi). The one reset spot to avoid is on Card 24.

**Source:** BFD 1.2 (resets, Jedi); HB 25.1–25.4; HN Handler Offense (Dump Swing); Flik L3 Lesson 7.

## Card 23 · Beating a good mark: threaten both sides

**Release:** week 16

**Why it matters.** Good teams will mark well and take away your favourite throw. Elite throwers stay dangerous on both sides, so the marker can’t take everything at once.

**Three key points**

1. **Catch in threat position.** As soon as you catch, set up as if you’re about to make a specific throw, so the marker has to choose. Or stay neutral, with two hands on the disc, so they have nothing to read.
2. **Break early.** A mark is most vulnerable while it’s still setting. Look to the break side early in the stall.
3. **After one break, look for the next.** A break should lead straight to a throw that gains yards, not a stop to admire it.

**Coaching cue:** “Make them choose.”

**Diagram:** a thrower in threat position with arrows showing the IO and around threats on either side of the marker.

**At training:** week 16 joint session, the one-crossover game. The thrower may bring the disc across their midline only once. A break without a pivot is a win for offence; with a pivot it’s a tie. If you only have 10 minutes a week for breaking practice, use this game.

**On your own:** with a partner, catch 30 passes and set straight into threat position, switching between the forehand and backhand side. Your partner calls which throw they think is coming.

**Check yourself:** What are the two ways to set up on the catch? When is a mark most vulnerable?

**Next level:** practise off-balance throws on purpose (bumped, mid-step), so a good mark can’t knock you out of your release. Card 8 adds the half-pivot throw and other quick releases.

**Source:** BFD ch. 1; HB 35.1; UAP Handler O and D (Wiggins); UAP Breaking the Mark (Saul); HN Flik (Scoring via Upfield Dump).

## Card 24 · Getting off the sideline

**Release:** week 21; re-shared in week 40. 2028 priority: retention on the sideline, where we lost too many discs last season.

**Why it matters.** The sideline is where defences trap us: they force towards the line, so the only open throws are down a narrow strip. Getting it off early, before the defence settles, is the difference between a reset and a turnover.

**Three key points**

1. **Get it off early.** Don’t spend four or five seconds looking upfield when you’re trapped. Move the disc before the defence is balanced.
2. **Know where to catch the reset.** Worst: behind the thrower and still on the line (the forbidden region, a last resort only). Better: upfield on the line, or backfield into space. Best: off the line and upfield.
3. **Punish the trap.** The thrower squares up to the middle. Cutters attack the break side and the middle, not the line.

**Coaching cue:** “Off the line, then attack.”

**Diagram:** a trapped thrower on the sideline, with the four reset spots marked from worst to best.

**At training:** week 21 joint session, the variable stall count game for escaping the sideline. Split sessions practise sideline power position and the sideline wrinkle.

**On your own:** sketch a trapped thrower and mark the four reset spots from worst to best. Then check your sketch against the card.

**Check yourself:** Which reset spot is best, and which is worst? Where do cutters attack when we’re trapped?

**Next level:** expect a good defence to call “shift” around stall 5 to shut the dump, so dump earlier. Card 25 covers what to do if you’re still stuck late in the stall.

**Source:** HB 36.3–36.4; HN Flik (Sideline Resets, Upfield Dump); HN Handler Situations; HN Excel Premium (Common Offensive Mistakes).

## Card 25 · Late-stall escapes: run away, dip, be obvious

**Release:** week 36; re-shared in week 40. Follow-up to Cards 21 and 24.

**Why it matters.** Even a good offence is sometimes still on the line at stall 6. Turnovers there usually come from handlers improvising different ideas at once. Two agreed escapes mean the thrower always knows where the next look is.

**Three key points**

1. **Run away after the upline.** After an upline cut, the reset turns about 3 m past the thrower and runs away for a floaty, leading pass off the line.
2. **The dip at stall 8–9.** A 2 m pop in front of the disc, as the last look.
3. **Be obvious, and decide early.** It’s better that everyone knows the pattern than handlers freestyling into a turnover. After a downfield throw, decide early where you’re going and get there, rather than sprinting blindly.

**Coaching cue:** “Obvious beats clever.”

**Diagram:** a trapped thrower with the two late escapes: the upline cut that turns 3 m past the thrower and runs away, and the 2 m dip in front of the disc.

**At training:** week 36 joint session, the retention refresher. Then week 40, the variable stall count game and the escape game.

**On your own:** walk both escapes with a cone as the thrower: upline, turn 3 m past it and run away; then the 2 m dip. Ten of each, until the footwork is automatic.

**Check yourself:** What is the dip, and when do you use it? Why should resets be obvious?

**Source:** collected from the Next level notes of Cards 21 and 24. HN Flik (Sideline Resets, Upfield Dump); HN Handler Situations; HN Handler Offense.

## Card 26 · Quick disc: give-and-go and the dish

**Release:** week 16; re-shared in week 22 and, for the 2s, in week 32

**Why it matters.** The give-and-go attacks space nobody is guarding. The mark sits on one side, downfield defenders cover the other, so a thrower who moves straight after the throw leaves their defender behind.

**Three key points**

1. **Move as you throw.** Start running after you release, ideally before the disc has left your hand. Be smooth, not rushed: if you’re getting called for travels, you’re rushing.
2. **Seal the lane.** After an open-side throw, step upfield in front of your marker so they can’t get between you and the return pass.
3. **Time the dish.** The handler cutting upfield goes just before the receiver catches, then decisively. Throw the dish to the gut and let their momentum do the rest; there’s no need to lead it.

**Coaching cue:** “Throw and go.”

**Diagram:** a give-and-go: A throws to B on the open side, steps upfield to seal the lane, and receives the return pass in power position.

**At training:** week 16 split session, dominator from the horizontal stack with give-and-go and early breaks. The 2s run give-and-go in 3v3 possession in week 32.

**On your own:** throw-and-go with a partner or against a wall: 20 throws, taking your first step as the disc leaves your hand. If your pivot foot moves before the release, slow down.

**Check yourself:** When do you start running? Where do you throw the dish?

**Drills:** [Tap-in (GRUT)](/sessions/drills?q=Tap-in) · [Leading pass drill (GRUT)](/sessions/drills?q=Leading+pass+drill)

**Next level:** learn four give-and-go moves: immediate, delayed, break-side, and the Sockeye move (after an around break, the receiver charges and dishes back).

**Source:** HB 34.2; HN Flik (Give and Go, Sealing the Lane, The Dish); HN Handler Offense (Give-Go Moves).

# Defend

## Card 27 · Our marks: triangle by default, flat on the call

**Release:** week 7; re-shared in week 9, when the flat mark is added, and in week 39

**Why it matters.** The mark is the first defender the offence has to beat. A mark that forces hard makes every other defender’s job easier. The first five seconds of pressure are what make throwers give up on the break side. In GRUT’s words: “You’re responsible for stopping half of the field.”

**Three key points**

1. **Marking base.** Feet slightly wider than shoulders, weight on the balls of your feet, knees over toes, hips back, chest open, arms wide, eyes on the thrower, not the disc. Always on your toes, ready to move fast in either direction.
2. **Triangle mark (default).** “Shown throw with hand, around with body, inside with shoulder.” Stop the shown throw, move your feet to stop the first pivot, drop deeper for the second, then recover. Make the thrower spend the stall pivoting instead of reading the field. Don’t slack off when you reach the mark: go into it at full intensity and try to win there.
3. **Hands with a job.** There are four throwing lanes, two above and two below your arms. Each hand covers a lane, so place them deliberately rather than waving. Know how your thrower likes to throw: if they throw low, keep your hands low, and take away their favourite throw first.

**Coaching cue:** “Take the dangerous throw first, then close.”

**Diagram:** the triangle mark from above: marker’s body, hand and shoulder against the shown throw, around and inside break, with the four lanes marked.

**At training:** week 7 joint session, Herd’s Forcing the Disc session, then 3-person marking: before each set the thrower names their favourite throw and the mark takes it away; the thrower rates the mark’s intensity 1–3. In week 16, the one-crossover marking game. Refresher in week 39.

**On your own:** three sets of 30 seconds in the marking base, shuffling side to side and reaching without shifting your weight. Stay on your toes the whole time.

**Check yourself:** What does each part of the triangle mark take away: hand, body and shoulder? Where are your eyes?

**Videos:** [Three marking tips (GRUT)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=U4SmIgLh9bM)

**Next level:** the flat mark (1s, on the call). Shoulders and hips parallel to the endzone, except on the trap. Close down on the first threatened throw, then stay within 3 m for the second. On the call, everyone downfield knows the open side has changed. Card 28 matches the mark to the stack and the thrower.

**Source:** UAP Marking (Wiseman); DK (triangle marking); BFD (flat mark); HN Practice Sessions 3; HN Point of Attack (Triangle Marking; Marking Vert and Ho Stack); GRUT (Amsterdam) marking video, filmed with Flik Ultimate.

## Card 28 · Marking 2: match the mark to the thrower

**Release:** week 39. Follow-up to Cards 27 and 43.

**Why it matters.** The triangle mark is our default, not the answer to every thrower. Small changes for the stack in front of you and the player holding the disc take away what this offence wants, not what an average one wants.

**Three key points**

1. **Mark by stack.** 45° against a vert stack, flatter against a horo, and straight up on the open-side sideline.
2. **Mark by matchup.** Back off tall throwers and crowd the favourite throw of quick ones. Against the very best, tell your team which break you’re stopping.
3. **Know it before the game.** Add each opponent’s favourite throws to the scouting card (Card 49), so every mark knows what to take away before the first pull.

**Coaching cue:** “Know the thrower, pick the mark.”

**Diagram:** three marks from above: 45° against a vert stack, flatter against a horo, and straight up on the open-side sideline.

**At training:** week 39 joint session, the marking refresher and the mark-call drill: the coach calls “triangle” or “flat” mid-stall and the mark changes at once.

**On your own:** watch one thrower for a whole game and write down their two favourite throws. Those are what you would take away.

**Check yourself:** How does the mark change against a vert stack, against a horo and on the open-side sideline? What do you tell your team when you mark the very best throwers?

**Source:** collected from the Next level notes of Cards 27 and 43. HN Point of Attack (Marking Vert and Ho Stack; Marking Matchups); GRUT (Amsterdam) marking video, filmed with Flik Ultimate.

## Card 29 · Defensive footwork: low, fast feet, pick your spot

**Release:** week 8

**Why it matters.** Defence isn’t about getting blocks. Shutting down a cut raises the stall count, and turnovers follow. Footwork is what lets you stay in position for the whole point.

**Three key points**

1. **Low and balanced.** Stay on the balls of your feet with your head up. As soon as you slow down, get low again. You react; you don’t act.
2. **Never turn your back on the disc.** Drop-step and open your hips. Build speed in stages: sidestep, then crossover, then sprint. Turn the way that keeps your player in sight, even if it’s slower.
3. **Choose the space you’re protecting.** Keep a buffer to it, like a string from your belly button to theirs, so you have time to react to whatever they choose.

**Coaching cue:** “Low, fast feet, head up, pick your spot.”

**Diagram:** a defender in back-shoulder position on a cutter, with the protected space shaded and the drop-step direction shown.

**At training:** week 8 joint session, Herd’s footwork session (Practice Sessions 1+2) and keeping-margins drills.

**On your own:** five minutes of drop-steps: start in your stance, drop-step and open your hips, sidestep, crossover, then sprint three steps. Ten each way.

**Check yourself:** Which way do you turn when your player changes direction? What is the buffer for?

**Next level:** use the bail step: run at 45°, turned at the waist and shoulders, head on a swivel, so you see the disc and your player. When you get beaten, plant, push and land towards the space you need to cover. Recovery is a skill; three compact steps beat a panicked chase.

**Source:** UAP Defensive Footwork (Matthews); DK; Flik L4 Lesson 6; HB Tr 22; HN Practice Sessions 1+2; HN Excel Premium (Bail Step, Defending Away to Under).

## Card 30 · Reading the thrower: react, don’t guess

**Release:** week 9

**Why it matters.** Good throwers tell you what they’re going to do with their eyes, hips and shoulders. Defenders who read those cues arrive early; defenders who guess get faked.

**Three key points**

1. **Watch the body, not the disc.** Eyes, hips and shoulders show the throw before the disc moves.
2. **React, don’t guess.** Hold your base until the thrower commits, then move. Only move your feet when they move theirs.
3. **Talk is defence.** The mark counts the stall loudly. The sideline and teammates call “up”, “break” and “strike”. Give information, not orders.

**Coaching cue:** “Eyes, hips, shoulders.”

**Diagram:** a thrower showing the three cues, with arrows to the throw each one predicts.

**At training:** week 9 joint session, the star drill and reactive shuffles, done early while fresh. Then silent points, where nobody can talk, followed by points where only the sideline can.

**On your own:** watch one thrower for five minutes of game film with the sound off. Pause before each throw and guess it from their eyes, hips and shoulders.

**Check yourself:** What three things show the throw before the disc moves? When do you move your feet?

**Next level:** break the thrower’s rhythm. Mark from motion with a jagged approach and defensive fakes, covering about 1.5 people-widths. You won’t block the best throwers, but you can make them use a different throw.

**Source:** BFD S&C (reaction work); UAP Handler D (Wiggins); HB App. B; HN Point of Attack (Marking from Motion); HN Amsterdam Conference (Elite Defense: Mental Shift).

## Card 31 · Downfield D: back shoulder, concede the under

**Release:** week 20, the defence trial

**Why it matters.** Our match defence protects the deep space first. A huck scores in one throw; an under cut only gains a few metres and still has to beat the next defender.

**Three key points**

1. **Stand on the back shoulder.** Position yourself on the cutter’s deep, open-side shoulder so the under is the only option. Keep your back to the open side.
2. **Follow the under with your hips.** When they cut under, drop your hips and stay with them. Don’t let them turn you around.
3. **Take away the first option, every time.** Make them take their second option, then their third. You don’t need blocks to play great defence; stalls and bad throws come from shut-down cuts. The elite target: stop the first option 95% of the time, the second 50% and the third 20%.

**Coaching cue:** “Deep first, then fight the under.”

**Diagram:** a cutter in a vert stack with the defender on the deep, open-side shoulder, the deep space shaded as “ours” and the under space as “conceded”.

**At training:** week 20 trial session, Tag Prestall and Blocks Only. 2s captains: tell your players which order they’re protecting. Some teams protect the open under first, then deep, then break.

**On your own:** watch one defender for five points of game film. Which shoulder do they stand on, and what do they give up?

**Check yourself:** Which space do we protect first, and why? Which shoulder do you stand on?

**See also:** Card 20, winning the deep ball, with video.

**Next level:** steal a look at the field when your player is stationary, has committed to a line, or is clearing. That’s heads-up D, and it’s what lets you poach and switch later.

**Source:** HN Next Gen 10 (downfield footwork); HN Team Defence (Heads-up D); HN 10 Big Drills (Win the Box); HN Amsterdam Conference (Elite Defense); Flik L4 Lesson 6.

## Card 32 · Transition: the first three seconds after a turn

**Release:** week 22

**Why it matters.** The moment possession changes, both teams are out of shape. The team that reorganises first gets the easiest goals and blocks of the game.

**Three key points**

1. **On offence: pick your target before you pick up the disc.** Look upfield first. Then take the free yards, move the disc, or wait and walk. Spread the field and clear the dead space within about 3 m of the disc.
2. **On defence: stop the easiest first throw.** Whoever is nearest marks. Everyone else picks up the most dangerous player near them. State the force twice, calmly.
3. **Call it early.** Cutters need four or five seconds to set up. The handler or sideline calls the play straight away.

**Coaching cue:** “Look, then pick up.”

**Diagram:** the moment after a turnover: the new thrower with the 3 m dead space cleared, cutters spreading, and defenders picking up the nearest dangerous player.

**At training:** week 22 joint session, quick disc processing (disc overload, five-pass game) and transitions. At the Solent warm-up, the three-team transition game.

**On your own:** in your next game of any kind, say the force out loud twice after every turnover, even if nobody else does.

**Check yourself:** On offence, what do you do before you pick up the disc? On defence, who marks?

**Next level:** a fast break isn’t always “throw immediately”. Sometimes the best option is to get the disc to your best thrower and isolate your best receiver. Avoid the two classic mistakes: rushing to pick up and getting stuck, or a half-speed pick-up that lets the defence set.

**Source:** HN Coach Corner (Fast Break Principles; The Key to Fast Break Offence); HN Handler Situations; UAP Handler O and D (Wiggins, Transitions).

## Card 33 · Poach and clog: defend space as a team

**Release:** week 24 (the idea is introduced in week 17); re-shared in week 33

**Why it matters.** An attacking flow usually involves three or four attackers and lasts about 20 seconds. The other defenders have time to help. Poaching and clogging let a team beat offences that are better one-on-one, which matters for both the 1s and the 2s.

**Three key points**

1. **Know when to leave.** Leave your player when someone is entering a space where they’ll probably score, your player is in a safe space, and you have a real chance to disrupt the play.
2. **Flash the lane.** Leave just long enough to deny one throw, then recover. Pick up the next player as soon as you leave the last.
3. **Everyone knows the plan.** If one person poaches, everyone shifts. Without a shared plan, poaching just looks lazy.

**Coaching cue:** “Clog their lane, then find your player.”

**Diagram:** a vert stack with one defender flashing into the open-side lane and the others shifting to cover.

**At training:** week 17 split sessions, team defence: which spaces are dangerous, and poaching the lanes. The 2s practise poach and clog near the disc.

**On your own:** watch one point of game film and follow a defender who isn’t near the disc. Pause each time you would leave your player, and ask the three questions.

**Check yourself:** What three things must be true before you leave your player? What does everyone else do when one person poaches?

**Next level:** against better offences, sit in the middle lanes and make them throw around you. Our person D isn’t beaten until they’ve completed the second throw. A poach that works makes the offence throw one more pass every possession.

**Source:** BFD (Defensive fundamentals); HN Flik (Why Poach, When to Poach, Flashing the Lane, Disruptive Poaching); HN Team Defence; HN Practice Sessions 11.

## Card 34 · Switching: call it early, call it by name

**Release:** week 33

**Why it matters.** Crossing cuts and picks are designed to beat one-on-one defence. A team that switches cleanly turns those plays into wasted cuts and can save a point when someone gets beaten deep.

**Three key points**

1. **Switch only on a clear call.** Switch on crossing cuts and picks when your teammate is closer to your player’s next space than you are. Call it early, use the player’s name, and both defenders commit.
2. **The caller decides.** Never switch onto a cutter already moving at full speed away from you. After a switch, new matchups hold until the next stoppage unless someone calls again.
3. **Help the helper.** When a teammate is beaten, your job is to make their mistake cost nothing.

**Coaching cue:** “Early, loud, by name.”

**Diagram:** two crossing cutters in a stack, with both defenders’ switch paths and the call shown.

**At training:** week 33 joint session, communication and switching (Help the Helper).

**On your own:** watch five crossing cuts on film. Pause as the cutters cross and say out loud who should call the switch, and when.

**Check yourself:** When do you switch? When should you never switch?

**Next level:** the buzz switch. When a cutter beats their defender deep, the nearest defender buzzes across to take them, and the beaten defender takes the open player. It’s perfect against handlers running a give-and-go. To spot switches, get your head up as your player changes direction and look downfield.

**Source:** HN Team Defence (Switching against cutters; downfield switching); HN Flik (Buzz Switch); Ian French (Help the Helper).

## Card 35 · Vert stack: from the back and the front

**Release:** week 5 (from the back); re-shared in week 6 (from the front) and week 22 (full version)

**Why it matters.** The vert stack is the 2s’ main offence and the 1s’ second one. It’s simple, keeps the lanes clear, and works in any wind.

**Three key points**

1. **The set-up.** A thrower, a dump and a stack starting about 10 m from the disc. Cuts happen in the lanes either side of the stack, never in it. The stack is where you recover.
2. **From the back (the 2s’ default).** One cutter, one clear. Time your cut to be open just before your teammate catches, and move the stack with the disc.
3. **From the front (with a third handler, “3H”).** Three handlers at the back; the front of the stack is an upfield reset. Cuts go away from the disc or across the pitch with offset lateral cuts. A lateral throw to the 3H gains yards and opens the break side.

**Coaching cue:** “Cut in the lane, recover in the stack.”

**Diagram:** two vert stacks side by side: cutting from the back (deep player cuts under, next clears) and from the front (3H lateral cut opening the middle channel).

**At training:** week 5 split sessions, vert from the back with continuation pairs. Week 6, vert from the front. Week 22, the 2s run vert in full.

**On your own:** set out coins as a vert stack, a thrower and a dump. Move the disc to each sideline in turn and reset the stack each time.

**Check yourself:** Where do cuts happen, and what is the stack for? In vert from the back, when should you be open?

**Next level:** use the undefended channel. When the front of the stack cuts laterally to the open side, the middle opens for a swing to the break side and a hit on the back of the stack. The first cut has to be real, or the defender won’t move.

**Source:** HN Flik (Vertical Stack, Upfield Dump, Undefended Channel); DK 10.5, 6.3; SP (3H cutting).

## Card 36 · Horo stack and the dominator

**Release:** week 15 (horo); re-shared in week 16 (dominator) and week 22 (full version)

**Why it matters.** The horo is the 1s’ main offence. It spreads the defence across the full width, so throwing long is easy and the break side is always live. The dominator gives our best handlers a way to beat poaching defences.

**Three key points**

1. **The set-up.** Three handlers, and four cutters spread across the width about 9 m apart, 8–12 m from the disc. Only the pair in front of the disc is active; the rest stay wide and out of the lanes.
2. **Use horo shapes, not vert shapes.** Away and under, and peppermills: the two in front of the disc cut equal and opposite, then take each other’s space.
3. **Dominator.** Two or three handlers work the disc upfield with short passes and give-and-goes while cutters clear vertically. One player gets every second pass. Catch and immediately fake or throw; after the throw, start running. Cutters time their entry for when the handlers stop, or the disc stalls.

**Coaching cue:** “Space decides the role.”

**Diagram:** a horo with the active pair marked and a peppermill shown; beside it, a dominator with handler give-and-go paths and cutters clearing deep.

**At training:** week 15 split session, horo with breaks to the active pair (break-throw bonus point). Week 16, dominator from the horo with give-and-go and early breaks.

**On your own:** set out coins as a horo: three handlers, and four cutters 9 m apart. Find the active pair with the disc in the middle and on each side, then walk a peppermill with them.

**Check yourself:** Which cutters are active in the horo? What do you do straight after a throw in the dominator?

**Next level:** against a deep poach, run the banana: both handlers go upline to open a big swing. Defences will poach off the furthest handler, so keep handler movement dynamic and the disc in the middle two-thirds of the pitch.

**Source:** HN Flik (Horizontal Stack, Peppermills and Banana, Handler Positioning); BFD (Dominator; “Alternate pistons”); HB 34.2; HN Team Analysis (PoNY 2021).

## Card 37 · Starting the point: pulls and pull plays

**Release:** week 23; re-shared in week 31 with Card 38

**Why it matters.** Every point starts with a pull. A great pull puts the offence in the worst possible position. A great pull play gets a free first cut before the defence is set.

**Three key points**

1. **Pull deep, with hang time.** Aim for the back of the endzone, or to roll out of the sideline, so the defence is set before the disc lands. Only aim for the brick if you can’t reach further.
2. **Chase as a unit.** Everyone knows the coverage. Chasers call early whether the disc is “central” or “sideline”.
3. **Receiving: “1” secures, “2” centres.** The 1 moves forwards onto the pull. The 2 checks their shoulder, gets easily open and centres it. About 70% of the time, the disc should then go upfield to a cutter quickly.

**Coaching cue:** “First look, then backup.”

**Diagram:** pull shapes from above (OI, IO, double helix) aimed diagonally across the pitch, plus the 1 and 2 positions for receiving.

**At training:** week 23 split sessions, pull plays and endzone basics. Week 31 joint session, the pulling game and the pull chase.

**On your own:** learn our pull plays from the playbook. For each one, write down the ideal output and the backup.

**Check yourself:** Where should a pull land? What do the 1 and the 2 do when we receive?

**Next level:** every pull play has an ideal output (for example, a free deep cut for your best hucker) and a backup. When it dies, swing into your system; don’t hang out in the deep space or clog the front of the stack. Pulling technique is on Card 38.

**Source:** HN Flik (Pulling Strategy, Shapes of Pull, Pulling Technique, Training Pulling); HN Handler Situations (Fielding the Pull, Being the 2); HN Excel Premium (Dynamic Pull Plays, Transitioning to your System).

## Card 38 · Pulling technique: a starter motor, not a discus

**Release:** week 31, with the pulling game. Follow-up to Card 37.

**Why it matters.** The pull is the one throw nobody marks, so it rewards technique more than any other. A repeatable pull with hang time lets the chase arrive before the disc is centred.

**Three key points**

1. **Pick a target.** Choose where the pull should land before you start, then use a relaxed run-up with a cross-step.
2. **Pull straight back.** “A starter motor, not a discus”: pull the disc straight back, then push hard through the front foot.
3. **Little and often.** Practise a few pulls at a time, including when you’re tired.

**Coaching cue:** “Starter motor, not discus.”

**Diagram:** a puller side-on, showing the cross-step, the disc pulled straight back, and the push through the front foot.

**At training:** week 31 joint session, the pulling game and the pull chase. Pullers also take a few pulls each way in every game-day warm-up.

**On your own:** ten pulls at the end of your next throwing session, when you’re already tired. Pick a target for each one and count how many land within 10 m of it.

**Check yourself:** What do you choose before you start your run-up? What does “a starter motor, not a discus” tell you to do with the disc?

## Card 39 · Endzone and Spread: scoring from 10 m

**Release:** week 23; re-shared in week 27

**Why it matters.** Space shrinks near the endzone, and decisions have to be quicker. Teams that convert from 10 m win close games. We have two calls, and everyone should know both.

**Three key points**

1. **“Endzone” (vert stack).** The first three players to arrive run to the back, the fourth stays at the front, and the other three handle. Come wide around the pitch rather than running through the lanes.
2. **“Spread”.** Three handlers work a dominator at the front of the endzone while the other four spread wide across the back. If their defenders sag in to clog the dominator, hit the spread receiver.
3. **Score before they recover.** After a short huck, try to score straight away. If you can’t, overload one side or isolate one cutter.

**Coaching cue:** “Come wide, set fast.”

**Diagram:** two endzone set-ups: “Endzone” as a vert stack, and “Spread” with three handlers at the front and four receivers wide across the back.

**At training:** week 23 split sessions, endzone basics. Week 27, Endzone and Spread after the Tour 1 review.

**On your own:** set out coins for both calls, “Endzone” and “Spread”. Say where you go if you’re the first, the fourth and the sixth player to arrive.

**Check yourself:** In “Endzone”, where do the first three players to arrive go? In “Spread”, who do we hit if the defenders sag in?

**Next level:** the first cut isn’t always the goal. Use the dump behind to score upfield or swing to the break side, and reset patiently rather than forcing. Our team stats track conversion rate, so we can see whether this is improving.

**Source:** HB 26.4–26.5; HN Excel Premium (Converting in the Redzone); HN Flik (Endzone Offence, Converting Short Hucks); Kieran (Spread).

## Card 40 · The Bull zone: make them swing, make them throw ugly

**Release:** week 24 (1s in full; the 2s play a basic version); re-shared in weeks 25 and 33

**Why it matters.** Herd’s 2-3-2 zone forces lots of swings that don’t gain distance and pushes the disc into congested spaces where aggressive players get blocks. In wind, it turns every extra throw into a chance for a turnover.

**Three key points**

1. **One job per role.**

| Role | Job |
| --- | --- |
| Two chases | Hold a middle force. Allow backward swings, but stop easy resets and close give-and-goes. |
| Bull | Sit just behind the chases (not close enough to double-team). Shut down every throw through the middle. |
| Wings | Stop shots over the top. The far wing pinches in as a second bull but doesn’t bite on the third handler. |
| Deep-deep and short-deep | Stop hucks. The deep-deep takes the deepest player; the short-deep pushes into the middle. |

2. **Zone is not half effort.** Run hard, attack the disc and hold your position all the time. Count the attackers in your area every time the disc moves.
3. **CRUMBLE.** The shout to switch to person defence. Echo it so everyone hears. Pick up the deepest offensive players first; when in doubt, leave the thrower unmarked.

**Coaching cue:** “Echo every call.”

**Diagram:** the 2-3-2 shape with each role labelled, then the same zone after a swing to the sideline (active wing up, far wing pinching in).

**At training:** week 24 split session (1s), the Bull zone forcing middle and the CRUMBLE call. Week 25, Blocks Only with a zone and zone by wind call.

**On your own:** draw the 2-3-2 from memory and label all seven roles. Then redraw it with the disc on a sideline.

**Check yourself:** What is the bull’s job? What does CRUMBLE mean, and who do we pick up first?

**Next level:** the bull’s job is to make the throw ugly, not to get the block; the blocks come from the wings and the middle reading the forced throw. A pop between the chases isn’t dangerous, so don’t chase it: shut down the next pass instead. A swing backwards is a win for the zone.

**Source:** HN Bull Zone; HN Flik (Zone Concepts, Transitions); BFD (zone counting).

## Card 41 · Arrowhead and the line trap

**Release:** week 25 (1s); re-shared in week 30 (2s preview) and week 39 (2s in full)

**Why it matters.** In a strong crosswind, the arrowhead is our sideline defence. It puts a wall in front of the disc so the offence has to make lots of small, safe passes to gain ground, and slow progress leads to bad decisions.

**Three key points**

1. **Wall in front of the disc.** Defenders block the forward throwing lanes and deny the middle. Swings are allowed; the job is not to be outflanked. Know the weakness: a patient offence can gain a few metres every swing and tire the zone.
2. **Force to the sideline.** Push the disc towards the line, especially downwind.
3. **Trap it on the line.** Once the disc reaches the sideline, the chase and two tips close the trap and take away the escape throws.

**Coaching cue:** “Wall up, don’t get outflanked.”

**Diagram:** the arrowhead shape in the middle of the pitch, then the line trap on the sideline with the chase and two tips marked.

**At training:** week 25 split session (1s), Bull zone or arrowhead by wind call. Week 30, arrowhead with a line trap and a wind plan.

**On your own:** draw the arrowhead in the middle of the pitch, then the line trap on the sideline. Mark the chase and the two tips.

**Check yourself:** What are swings allowed to do, and what must never happen? Who closes the line trap?

**Next level:** add the Japanese poach. When the disc swings to a reset, the nearest arrowhead defender first poaches into the lane to cut off continuation, and only then sets the mark once the zone has adjusted. They must turn and look behind them as the disc swings.

**Source:** HN Flik (Arrow Head; 3-3-1 Japanese Poach); Flik Zone Concepts 6.

## Card 42 · Zone offence: around, through, over

**Release:** week 27 (around and through); re-shared in week 34 (over)

**Why it matters.** Teams will zone us in wind. A zone wants us to throw sideways until we make a mistake. Knowing the three ways to beat it turns a zone into our chance to score.

**Three key points**

1. **Around.** Four handlers in a bucket keep the full width. Swing with an OI curve, and catch and throw in one motion so the cup (the defenders around the thrower) can’t catch up.
2. **Through.** One handler (the controller) gets every second pass. Crash the cup: move into the space inside it for a short pass to reset the stall, gain yards or pull defenders in. If a crash isn’t thrown to, get out so you don’t crowd the thrower.
3. **Drive, then decide.** If passes through the middle gain yards, keep going. If the middle gets congested, the zone has compressed, so dump and swing around it.

**Coaching cue:** “Swing it, crash it, or go over it.”

**Diagram:** a cup zone with three arrows: around (bucket swing), through (crash into the cup) and over (hammer into the space behind).

**At training:** week 27 split session, controller, popper and crash; El Rondo; 3v2. Week 34 (1s), going over: hammers and blades.

**On your own:** watch five minutes of any team playing against a zone. Tally each completed pass as around, through or over.

**Check yourself:** What are the three ways to beat a zone? What do you do if you crash the cup and don’t get the disc?

**Next level:** go over on purpose. Only throw a hammer when the target is open behind the cup, you have a clean release, and it won’t float into a wing. Blades and scoobers get over quicker; hammers go further. Deep cutters drag the deeps back to open the middle.

**Source:** HN Flik (Dump and Swing, Crashing the Cup, Zone Offence); Flik Zone Offence 1–3, 8, 9; HB 31.2, 33.3.

## Card 43 · Force middle and point-of-attack options

**Release:** week 33 (1s); re-shared in week 39 for the marking calls

**Why it matters.** Good teams adapt to one defence. A few extra looks from the mark let us take away the opposition’s favourite thing without changing our whole system.

**Three key points**

1. **Force middle (1s option).** The mark forces the thrower towards the middle, and downfield defenders clog it. It self-corrects on both sides of the pitch and works well against big, fast teams. Call it early, from the mark and the sideline.
2. **Anti-huck force.** Line your centre up on the thrower’s hucking shoulder with your arms high. You’ll give up short breaks; that’s the trade. Bladey throws are fine; flat IO hucks are not.
3. **No-hammer mark.** Flash a hand into the hammer zone. When you see a hammer wind-up, push your chest up and hand high, knees bent, ready for the next break. The aim isn’t a block, just a higher, floatier hammer.

**Coaching cue:** “Take their favourite throw.”

**Diagram:** a pitch in force middle with the marks pointing inward from both sideline positions and downfield defenders clogging the centre.

**At training:** 1s split sessions from week 33: the jam-the-middle drill (two handlers and a stack on the middle line; the force is called on every throw) and the anti-huck drill (handler, mark and cutter). Week 39 joint session: the mark-call drill, for the marking calls.

**On your own:** watch one opponent on film and decide which of the three looks you’d call against them, and why.

**Check yourself:** What do you give up with the anti-huck force? What is the aim of the no-hammer mark?

**Next level:** measure force middle. There should be at least one poach look per point, the opposition’s yards should come mainly from break throws, and they should need about three more passes per point than against a one-way force. Card 28 covers adjusting the mark to the matchup.

**Source:** HN Point of Attack (Force Middle; Anti-Huck Force; Marking against Hammer; Marking Matchups).

## Card 44 · Good throw versus good decision

**Release:** week 19, before the second trial; re-shared in week 22 with the retention block

**Why it matters.** Most turnovers come from the choice, not the release. A perfect throw to a covered player is still a turnover; a scrappy throw to the right player usually isn’t.

**Three key points**

1. **Only throw to players who are free.** Free means separation, the right hips, and a lane, not just distance from the defender.
2. **Every touch has a job.** Gain yards, change the point of attack or break the mark. If the throw does none of these and isn’t safe, hold it and dump.
3. **Judge decisions apart from execution.** After a turnover, ask two questions: was it the right option? Was the throw good? Fix the one that went wrong.

**Coaching cue:** “Right option first, then the right throw.”

**Diagram:** a 2×2 grid with good and bad decisions on one axis and good and bad execution on the other, with an example in each box.

**At training:** week 19 trial session, the breaking and reading games. In film sessions, pause before the throw and ask: what would you do next?

**On your own:** keep your own decision grid all season: the situations where you turn it over and what you’ll try instead. Bring it to your mid-season and end-of-season reviews.

**Check yourself:** What makes a player free? What two questions do you ask after a turnover?

**Next level:** know the risk-reward of the score and the clock. A 50/50 huck at 14–14 in wind is a different decision from the same huck at 3–1.

**Source:** DK’s Bible 6.11; BFD (performance model); HN Excel Premium (Common Mistakes; Fixing Focus); Season plan (Measuring improvement).

## Card 45 · Process over outcome

**Release:** week 14, alongside classroom session C4

**Why it matters.** “Winning Nationals” isn’t something you can do on a Wednesday night. Outcome goals set the direction, but process goals give you feedback every session, which is how you actually get better.

**Three key points**

1. **Outcome goals point the way.** Nationals for the 1s, winning more games for the 2s, a starting O-line spot for you. They’re the destination, not the plan.
2. **Process goals can be hit many times a session.** For example: check your shoulder 5–6 times a point; give sideline talk three times a point; make the opposition throw 10 passes before they score.
3. **Rank your priorities.** Know the team’s top three for the season, in order, and be clearer about them than feels necessary.

**Coaching cue:** “What can you do on a Wednesday night?”

**Diagram:** a ladder with the season outcome goal at the top and three process goals as the rungs.

**At training:** in C4, each small group ranks the draft team priorities. Then everyone sets one personal process goal for the next month and shares it with a buddy.

**On your own:** write your process goal on your water bottle or your phone’s lock screen. Count it at the next three sessions.

**Check yourself:** What is the difference between an outcome goal and a process goal? How often should you be able to hit a process goal?

**Next level:** track your process goals. A goal you can’t measure gives no data. Pair each one with a number you can count at training, such as shoulder checks per point or breaks per six points.

**Source:** HN Amsterdam Conference (Process over Outcome; Army of Dump Defenders); The Culture Code (Coyle).

## Card 46 · Performing under pressure

**Release:** week 17, alongside classroom session C5; re-shared in week 18 as trials start

**Why it matters.** Trials, universe points and Regionals all bring nerves, and that’s normal. Players who have a routine for pressure play closer to their best when it matters most.

**Three key points**

1. **“I’m not nervous, I’m excited.”** The energy is the same; only the label changes. Treat pressure as a challenge, not a threat.
2. **Build a 10-second pre-point routine.** One breath, one cue word, and one picture of a good rep. Use the same routine every point.
3. **Accept and refocus.** Notice the nerves or the mistake, accept them, then bring your attention back to your next job. Don’t try to make the feeling go away.

**Coaching cue:** “Loose, but serious.”

**Diagram:** the routine as three steps in a row: breathe → cue word → picture.

**At training:** in C5, each player builds their routine, then does a 5-minute guided visualisation of a trial game going well. Use the routine at every trial.

**On your own:** write your 10-second routine down: one breath, one cue word, one picture. Run it before ten throws this week.

**Check yourself:** What three things go into the pre-point routine? What do you do with nerves: fight them or accept them?

**Next level:** focus on what you control. Aim for relaxed concentration (fully focused but loose) and accept that some arousal helps. At trials, assessors note good decisions after mistakes, not just the mistakes.

**Source:** The Inner Game of Ultimate Frisbee (Adams); HN Coach Corner (Getting out of your head); Flik Case Studies (Ring 2021); HN Case Studies (PoNY 2018).

## Card 47 · Sideline talk and sideline buddies

**Release:** week 9, with the silent points; re-shared in week 23, when buddies are set for the Solent warm-up

**Why it matters.** At a tournament, more than half the squad is on the sideline at any time. A sideline that talks well is an extra set of eyes for every player on the pitch.

**Three key points**

1. **Have a buddy.** Each player on the line has a buddy on the sideline, ideally in a similar role, who watches their matchup and talks to them.
2. **Give information, not orders.** “Up!”, “Break!”, “Strike!”, “Your player’s cutting under.” Short, specific and early. Echo zone calls and the force so the whole defence hears them.
3. **Calm and specific after a point.** Use ready → situation → what you did → what to try → check. One idea, not a list. Notice and name improvement out loud: specific praise moves players more than criticism.

**Coaching cue:** “Tell them what you see.”

**Diagram:** none needed. A short list of the standard calls (up, break, strike, huck, stall, CRUMBLE) would help.

**At training:** week 9 joint session, silent points and then sideline-only points. At the Solent warm-up (week 23), every player has a buddy for the day.

**On your own:** watch one point of any game as if you were the sideline buddy of one defender. Say your calls out loud: short, specific and early.

**Check yourself:** What is the difference between information and an order? What are the five steps for talking to a player after a point?

**Next level:** set a sideline process goal, such as three useful calls per point.

**Source:** BFD (Team management; Training the sideline); Flik Zone Concepts (communication points); Hildo Bijl (feedback steps); HN Amsterdam Conference (Process over Outcome).

## Card 48 · Tournament day: the Herd event routine

**Release:** week 22, the week before the Solent warm-up; re-shared before every event

**Why it matters.** The same routine at every tournament means less to think about and more energy for the games. By the end of the season, both squads should run it without coaches leading it.

**Three key points**

1. **Before: know the plan.** Read the briefing: objectives, process goals, lines and roles, opponents, logistics. Know the “what if” plan (injured handler, broken twice, scored on three in a row). In heat, plan water, shade, sunscreen and food between games.
2. **During: same warm-up, same jobs.** Run the Herd game-day warm-up, finishing with the 4v3 mini; pullers take a few pulls each way. The stats owner records score, O/D start, blocks and turnovers every point. Sideline buddies talk. Tell the opposition we intend to play a great-spirited game.
3. **After: review in five minutes.** After each game: what did we intend, what happened, why, what will we keep, what will we change? At the end of the event, the captains agree the top two fixes for the next week.

**Coaching cue:** “Game by game, not for the prize.”

**Diagram:** the routine as a one-page checklist (before, warm-up, during, after each game, end of event); print it for the team bag.

**At training:** run the full routine at the Solent warm-up (week 23) and judge the day on process goals, not results.

**On your own:** pack your bag from a list the night before, and read the briefing twice: once when it arrives and once on the way there.

**Check yourself:** What five questions make up the after-game review? What four things does the stats owner record every point?

**Drills:** [Rinkydink (GRUT)](/sessions/drills?q=Rinkydink)

**Next level:** know the timeout rules. Save them for preventing turnovers at high stall, not after a lone huck. Keep them to one message and one play. Straight after the opposition’s timeout, expect a set play: double-team the reset or poach the nearest lane for three seconds.

**Source:** HN Case Studies (Mooncatchers 2024); HN Coach Corner (Preparing for Pressure; Time Outs; Base Metric Analysis; Practicing Spirit); BFD.

## Card 49 · Scouting and game plans

**Release:** week 38, alongside classroom session C9; scouting cards are re-shared in week 42 for Regionals

**Why it matters.** CUSB put their 2019 EUCF result down to scouting: a team of scouts, two on every game, writing down every player’s favourite throws and cuts. Knowing what’s coming lets us take away what an opponent likes most.

**Three key points**

1. **One-page scouting card.** Their best players and favourite throws and cuts; their main offence and defence; what they do after a timeout; what they do in wind.
2. **Plans A, B and C.** Know them before the game, built from our team, their team, the context and the conditions. Each plan has a one-word call.
3. **Know the triggers.** On defence, switch after two easy holds in a row, or if they’re scoring in fewer than five passes. On offence, switch after two turnovers in a row from the same problem. The captains decide at a timeout or between points.

**Coaching cue:** “Know the plan, hear the word.”

**Diagram:** a decision flow: Plan A → trigger → Plan B → trigger → Plan C, with the one-word call on each arrow.

**At training:** in C9, scout one clip together using the card template, then pairs of scouts take an opponent each. Cards are due in week 40 and printed for the sideline at Regionals. Week 41’s rehearsal triggers Plans B and C at least once each. The 1s set O- and D-lines by week 40, so stats can be split by line.

**On your own:** fill in a one-page scouting card for one team from any game film: best players, favourite throws and cuts, main offence and defence.

**Check yourself:** What goes on the one-page scouting card? What are the triggers to change plan on defence?

**Next level:** scout in pairs so you get two opinions on the same game. Then train how to escape the situations the opponent creates, not just how to recognise them.

**Source:** HN Case Studies (CUSB 2019; Mooncatchers scouting sheet); BFD (Game plans); HN Coach Corner (Base Metric Analysis).
