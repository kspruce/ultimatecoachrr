The Herd way

Have fun together. Get better every session. Become a team nobody wants to draw, and enjoy getting there.

Everything in this handbook hangs off three ideas, in this order. The order matters: each one is built on the one before it.

1

A team people want to be part of

Fun is the foundation, not the reward for winning. People who enjoy turning up keep turning up. People who keep turning up get better. And a group that trusts each other plays braver ultimate.

2

Every session makes everyone better

The 1s and the 2s, the newest rookie and the longest-serving veteran. We judge sessions by whether people improved, not by who looked good, and we track it with benchmarks and simple process goals.

3

A force over time

Strong teams are built over seasons, not weekends. We learn one big idea at a time (the strategic anchor for each phase), keep the ones that work and stack the next on top.

What we ask of every leader

  • Play hard. Your effort sets the floor for everyone else's. You don't have to play well to lead well.
  • Look after people. Know everyone's name, notice who's quiet, and make newcomers feel they belong from their first session.
  • Keep it fun. Games first, laughter allowed, and a reason to stay for the social.
  • Keep it simple. One idea at a time, three points at most.
  • Make a decision. It won't always be the right one. Make it, own it and learn from it.

How we'll know it's working

  • People come back: session attendance, and how many return next season.
  • Everyone touches the disc and everyone improves, which the benchmark rounds in October, at trials, in May and in September will show.
  • Opponents enjoy playing us. Spirit scores, and how games feel.
  • Results, last. They follow the other three.

Captaining

What great captains do

Sam Walker studied the most dominant teams in sport. What they had in common wasn't a superstar or a genius coach. It was a particular kind of captain.

Those captains were rarely the most talented player, rarely the most charismatic, and almost never gave speeches. What they shared were seven habits, and every one of them can be learned.

1

Relentless effort

They never coast: not in a drill, not 13–3 up. A team drifts to the effort level of whoever sets the floor.

At Herd: first to the cones, full speed in the 4v3 mini, still sprinting on the last point of the day.

2

Playing to the edge

Controlled intensity that pushes right up to the line without crossing it.

At Herd: in a self-refereed sport, the line includes Spirit. Compete hard, call fair, never make it personal.

3

Doing the thankless jobs

They carry the kit, take the line nobody wants and mark the player nobody wants to mark. Serving the team earns the right to push it.

At Herd: cones, discs, the deep pull chase, picking up the stragglers in warm-up.

4

Constant, practical talk

Not speeches. A steady stream of short, useful words: names, praise, information, especially to quiet players and newcomers.

At Herd: talk to every player at every session, even if it's one sentence.

5

Showing it without words

Moments that change the mood: a sprint back on defence, picking a teammate up off the ground, a high-five line.

At Herd: when a point goes badly, be the first one jogging back to the line.

6

Standing apart when it matters

Willing to disagree about how the team plays, and to stand up for a teammate. Never personal.

At Herd: disagree in the leaders' chat or the huddle, then back the decision in front of the team.

7

A switch for emotions

Frustration happens. Great captains switch it off fast, so it doesn't spread through the team.

At Herd: have a reset you practise, like one breath and a cue word, before the next pull.

What doesn't make a captain

Being the most talented, the most charismatic, the best at speeches or the one who looks the part. Teams pick on these all the time, and they're poor predictors.

Captains and coaches

The best set-ups treat the captain as the coach's partner on the pitch. The coach plans and teaches; the captain carries it onto the field and tells the coach honestly how the team is feeling.

Try this

  • After a session, give yourself 1–3 on each of the seven habits. Pick one to work on next week.
  • When choosing captains and player leaders, describe these seven habits and ask the team who shows them. Don't ask who's best.

Leaders people follow

Leadership isn't a personality type. It's a relationship between a leader and the group, and it's only as good as that relationship.

We're bad at spotting leaders: we tend to pick the loud, confident one. What actually works is a leader who understands what the team cares about and acts on it. Four things, best worked on in this order:

1. Be one of us

Live the team's values yourself. Keep listening, especially when things are going well, because that's when leaders stop. Chat to people, ask for feedback and wear the kit.

2. Champion the team

Turn individual moments into team ones: "that layout block came from everyone working hard on defence today." Never make a win about you. Be fair to everyone, and let the players set the standards for training and attendance at the start of the season, so you're holding them to their own rules.

3. Tell the story

Keep saying who we are and where we're going. What makes Herd, Herd? A fun, welcoming club that's getting better every season is a story people want to join.

4. Build it

Make what we do match what we say. For a mixed club that means drills, pull rotation, leadership roles and language that involve everyone equally.

Try this

  • Write down what you think Herd's values are. Then ask five players. Be ready to hear something different, and lead the team you find, not the one you imagined.
  • Next time someone does something great, say it to the group and link it to the team.

Sharing the load

Nobody can run a club alone, and nobody should try. Teams that spread leadership are more committed, more confident and play better.

Sharing leadership doesn't make captains look weaker. Leaders who hand out responsibility are seen as better leaders, not worse ones. It also gets more people invested: a player with a job cares more about the team.

Choosing player leaders

  • Ask the players. Coaches and captains are often poor at spotting who the team already follows.
  • Describe the qualities a role needs and ask who fits, rather than running a popularity vote.
  • Keep roles informal where you can. People take on "can you lead warm-ups this month?" more easily than a title.
  • Give experienced players a job. It puts their experience to work for the team.
  • Be clear what each role covers, when, and why you're sharing it.

Fun is the foundation

Everyone at Herd is a volunteer with a job, studies and a life. They come because they enjoy it. Protect that.

Motivation that comes from enjoying the game lasts. Motivation that comes from outside (selection, trophies, approval) is powerful but brittle: players driven mostly by it burn out sooner and enjoy it less.

People stay motivated when a team gives them three things:

  • A say: some control over what they do. This one matters most for improving.
  • Progress: feeling capable and getting better.
  • Belonging: feeling connected to the people around them.

In sessions

  • Ask before you tell. Involve players in goals and tactics, and let them set their own targets.
  • Build choice into drills. In a cutting drill, don't put out cones for where to cut. Let players decide how to get free.
  • When there's no choice, explain why. "This footwork drill is how you get open in week 4."
  • Say which mistakes are fine today. "Drops are fine if you attacked the disc."
  • Limit ranking. Keep head-to-head comparison to about a third of a session at most. The rest is about everyone getting better.
  • Plan the fun in. Games first, the 4v3 mini, fun consequences in scrimmages, the festive tournament.

Before every session, ask

  • Why are we doing each drill, and how will I explain it?
  • Where can players make a choice?
  • Have I balanced competitive games with time to learn?
  • Which mistakes are okay today, and have I said so?

Building a fun team

Who we are

The more players feel the team is theirs, the more they turn up early, volunteer, back each other and hold their nerve under pressure.

A team's identity is what it values plus the behaviours that show it. Leaders can't hand it down. It only sticks if the team builds it together, and that's half the point: the process itself brings people together.

How to find it

  1. Tell the team why it matters. Two minutes at the start is enough.
  2. Ask everyone: "What words describe us when we're at our best?" Each person brings two or three.
  3. In small groups, merge the lists. "Hard work", "graft" and "never give up" are probably one value.
  4. Vote down to three to five values. Sticky dots on a board work well.
  5. For each value, agree what it looks, sounds and feels like at training and at tournaments. This step is what makes it real.

Be ready for a different answer from the one you expected. Lead the team you discover.

What a value looks like in practice

Say the team chooses joy. Behaviours might be: we celebrate a 2s block as loudly as a 1s goal; every session ends with a game; nobody leaves the social without being talked to.

Living it every week

Values on a slide don't change anything. What changes a team is hearing them, seeing them and doing them every week.

Say it

Use the values' own words when you praise people. If commitment is a value, say "great commitment chasing that pull", not "nice one". Put them in the kit, the socials and the team chat.

Build it in

Make the club's routines match who we say we are: socials, rituals, how we pick teams in drills. Collect the things that matter to us, like a photo wall of good times, a running tally of people who came through the 2s, or a ridiculous mascot that comes to every tournament. If you ever have to say "because I said so", the change probably doesn't fit.

Take it outside

Show it beyond the team: coach at taster sessions, help juniors, wear the kit around town and post about the club.

Check it

Ask again mid-season whether we still value the same things. Judge the season by our values, not only results. Watch for cliques: they're the biggest threat to a team feeling like one team.

Building a fun team

Time together

The teams that win big usually aren't the ones with the slickest vert stack. They're the ones that put time into each other off the pitch.

You can't drill trust, and you can't measure it, but you can feel it on a big point. It's built in small, low-effort moments, repeated over seasons.

Easy wins

  • A kickabout, food or a coffee after training. Say clearly that everyone is invited, every time.
  • Use tournament downtime together: meals, travel, the gaps between games. Plan a few loose team activities so nobody sits brooding, but leave people space to look after themselves.
  • Mix it up. Throw with someone different, train in different groups, sit with someone new.
  • Share the solo work, like a chat channel where people post their workouts.
  • Silly team traditions. They're part of what people remember.

Two training habits that build trust

  • No "sorry" at training. Nobody drops a disc on purpose, so nobody needs to apologise.
  • No bailout calls in practice. If you threw into triple coverage and got fouled, the turn stands. It keeps us honest about good decisions.

Quick team activities

Set a shared goal together; swap roles for a drill (handlers cut, cutters handle); or set a problem-solving challenge in small groups.

Own the moment

Things go wrong in every game. A fun team decides in advance that the awkward moments are part of the plan.

Honest and positive

When a loss hurts, say so; don't pretend it didn't. Then point the team forward: more chances to play together and get better together.

Steer the energy

Nerves and excitement feel the same in the body. "I'm not nervous, I'm excited" keeps the energy and changes its direction.

Make it part of the plan

One US club got booed by opponents, so it started booing itself. When it happened in a game it was "all part of the plan". The same idea works elsewhere: celebrate your own timeouts as a joke, or make "we come from behind" part of who you are. Then going down a break is no big deal.

Talk about the process

Outcome goals say how we'll judge the season. Process goals say what we do every week: a well-attended session, game intensity, a social after training, events everyone can afford. Spend the season talking about the process.

  • Pick one Herd "part of the plan" ritual for when we go down a break: a chant, a call, a silly handshake. Let the team choose it.

Every player matters

The better your relationship with a player, the better they'll play, the faster they'll improve and the more they'll enjoy it. Don't leave it to chance.

Leading a big squad means juggling playing time, disagreements and everyone's own goals. Good relationships make all of that easier, so build them early on purpose rather than waiting until one goes wrong.

What a good relationship has

  • Closeness: trust, respect and genuine care.
  • Commitment: both of you want to keep working together.
  • Working together: clear roles, with the leader leading and the player engaged.
  • Seeing it the same way: you'd both describe the relationship similarly.

How

  • Make time one-to-one, away from training. Listen first.
  • Show belief: "You'll get that huck. Your release is already better than last month."
  • Empathy before fixing. If someone's frustrated, ask what's going on and let them be frustrated. Then ask how you can help.
  • Deal with problems quickly. Worrying about where they stand with a captain eats into a player's game.
  • Look after yourself. You can't fix everything, and you don't have to.

Building a fun team

Spirit is part of the fun

Play hard, play fair. Good Spirit isn't a game with no calls. It's a game where calls are handled so well that both teams enjoy it.

Before the game

Set the intention out loud: we want this to be the best-spirited game the other team has played. Say it at the flip, between spirit captains, and when we shake hands.

Making calls

  • Call what affects the play. Mention small things after the point: "I didn't call it, but you travelled a bit there."
  • Be consistent, whatever the score and whoever we're playing.
  • Never make a call planning to take it back.

When a call goes against you

  • Take a breath. Assume they're trying to follow the rules and believe their call.
  • Aim to settle it in under 45 seconds.
  • "Contest" is fine. It just means two honest views don't match.

Being spirited

Celebrate their great plays. Help the layout-blocker up and say "wow". Agree simple hand signals so the sideline can say "stand by it" or "think about taking it back" without shouting onto the field.

After the game

Note calls and discussions during the game so the spirit score is based on what happened, and agree the score before the spirit circle.

Building a fun team

Freedom to fail

Players only try new things if they know a mistake won't cost them their place, their confidence or their friends.

  • Say it, and mean it: failing while trying the right thing is fine.
  • Show what good looks like. In reviews, find the moments the team did what we wanted, even on points we lost.
  • Don't celebrate lucky plays. A low-percentage throw that came off is still a low-percentage throw. A good decision that didn't work is still a good decision.
  • Have each other's backs. When someone has a bad game, the team picks them up. A player who knows that will play better in the next one.
  • Expect hard moments. Every team has them: frustration, arguments, a player who wants to walk off. What matters is how the group handles them together. Teams that come through them come out closer.

Sessions that make everyone better

How we coach

Think about who you're coaching and why they came. Then tell, show and let them do it, with three points at most.

Start with the people

Why do our players play? What do they want from a session, and from you? The answers may differ between the 1s and the 2s. Plan with that in mind, keep the atmosphere positive, and use the mistakes you see to decide what to work on next.

Tell, show, do

People learn in different ways, and most need all three:

  • Tell: a clear, short explanation. No more than three key points per topic.
  • Show: a whiteboard with magnets (less scribble than pens), or experienced players walking it through.
  • Do: everyone runs it at half speed first, then builds up pace and intensity.

How skills develop

  1. Thinking: building a picture of the movement.
  2. Practising: repeating it, noticing errors, correcting them.
  3. Automatic: done without thinking. Habits here are hard to change, which is why fixing a long-time player's throw takes patience.

Plan, do, review

Plan each session, run it, then note quickly how it went: did the new idea land, how far did people get? Use that to plan the next one. Plan at least two sessions ahead, so you can pull the next one forward if the team flies. Give individual feedback soon after; a short message helps people who found it hard.

How you sound

Your words and body language set the tone. Ask open questions. Keep feedback positive: criticism is fine when it's clearly a step towards getting better.

Feedback that sticks

Short, specific, and something they can copy straight away. Then get excited when they do it in a game.

A four-step fix in a drill

  1. Start with something real they're doing well. "Lovely wrist snap, and everyone's stepping out."
  2. Make the correction a question. "Why would I step out like this rather than like this?"
  3. Show it, and have them copy it. Then let them practise while you walk round.
  4. Ask for it. "I want to see those step-outs for the next two minutes."

And when someone does it for real in a game, make a big deal of it. Nothing locks in learning like seeing the coach light up.

Specific beats general

"Good cutting!" tells nobody anything. "Great timing on that endzone cut: you waited for the swing before going" tells everyone what to repeat.

Bigger feedback

Keep the four steps for quick technical fixes. For anything bigger, separate praise from correction: give praise loudly and on its own, and have corrections as a two-way conversation, ideally one-to-one, starting by asking whether they want feedback.

Sessions that make everyone better

The sweet spot

People learn fastest when a task is just beyond what they can do now: hard enough to stretch them, not so hard they can only fail.

Too easy

Bored faces, chatting, sloppy mistakes from switched-off players, or everything going perfectly.

Too hard

A turnover on almost every rep, or messy mistakes because there's too much to process.

Just right

Focused players; mistakes, but not every rep; mistakes dropping as they get used to it and as you help.

Three ways to get them there

  • Adjust the challenge. You won't judge it right first time. Make it a bit easier or harder, then let them work it out.
  • Ask questions that point them towards the answer.
  • Tell them one thing to do. A concrete step or technique.

Example: chaining cuts

The team can cut 1v1, and now needs to link cuts. In a drill, the second cutter just sprints after the first catch. Make it easier by leaving the first cut unguarded; ask "where should you be, based on where the disc goes?"; or tell them "set up across the field before the first catch". In a 5v5 game with a bonus for cutter-to-cutter passes, everyone mobs the disc. Pair cutters so only A-to-B scores the bonus; ask how they can organise so only one person cuts at a time; or tell them "the nearest break-side cutter goes next".

Sessions that make everyone better

Planning a session

One clear aim per session, and a plan that builds on the last one.

Pick the aim first

List what the season needs to cover, give each session one focus, and use what happened at the last session or tournament to choose it.

Four kinds of session

KindWhat makes it work
Learning something newSlower pace, fresh legs, time for questions. Don't assume anything is obvious. Keep fatigue low: people slip back into old habits when they're tired or under pressure.
Grooving existing skillsThe most common kind. Few drills, repeated often, with small variations (switch the force, add a defender, change the cut angle). Give it a theme, like breaking the mark, and look for it in the closing game.
Fixing a problemUsually straight after a tournament. Break the problem into parts, drill them, then start games in the problem situation (trapped on the sideline, against a zone) and practise getting out.
Just playingStill purposeful: small-sided games for more touches, short stall counts for quicker decisions, 4v3 or 7v6 to practise poaching.

Practicalities

  • Short sessions (90 minutes or less): fewer parts, one focus, always a warm-up and cool-down.
  • Long sessions: blocks of an hour at most, with breaks for food, drink and a mental rest. It's also easier to share between coaches.
  • Warm, dry evenings suit explaining new ideas; cold or wet ones need drills where everyone keeps moving.
  • Splitting by ability isn't patronising. Beginners need lots of throws, not a game where they touch the disc twice.
  • Fitness: for bonding, any time with partner work; for improvement, at the start when fresh; for mental toughness, at the end; to practise playing tired, before a game.

Running and designing drills

Set the scene, show it three ways, start simple, stop before they're bored, and make it a competition.

Introducing a drill

  • Context: "We're trapped on the sideline."
  • Aim: "Get it off the line and swing to the far side."
  • Up to three focus points: "Fake to move the mark, lead the receiver, swing at the first chance."
  • Show it three ways: say it, draw it (whiteboard or cones), then have experienced players demonstrate. That also shows newcomers who the senior players are.
  • Everyone walks it at half speed. Add defenders or pace only once it flows.

Running it well

  • Start simple, build up. Add continuation throws or defenders as confidence grows.
  • Keep it short. About 20 minutes; less for a one-cut, one-throw drill. Stop before boredom.
  • Repeat across weeks. Once teaches an idea; habits need repetition. Resets and deep throws belong in most sessions.
  • Make it competitive. Points for offence and defence, or "beat the other group's score".
  • Individual or team? Team drills only work if individual skills are good enough. Don't give strong players throw-in-pairs time they could spend on game-like drills.

Designing your own

  1. Name the scenario. A real problem the team wants to solve, and how players will know they're succeeding. Some drills are meant to cause turnovers; say so.
  2. Break it down. Remove a defender or attacker, take away time pressure, shorten throws, throw from standing, use coloured cones or call when to cut.
  3. Build it up. Add one element at a time until it's game-like.

Make it real

  • Put players where they'd be in a game. A deep-throw drill should start from the stack, not level with the disc.
  • Finish the rep: catch, stop and face upfield (later, add a fake, then a continuation throw).
  • Add defenders as soon as the pattern is comfortable, with rules so they defend realistically.
  • Maximise live discs: queues ready, the next rep starting while the last is still going, rotations that keep people out of each other's way.
  • Simplify in wind or rain. If in doubt, start with the simplest version: it's easier to step up than to step back.

Sessions that make everyone better

Better decisions

The first question for any throw: can I reliably make it? After that it's risk against reward, and knowing when to push and when to play safe.

Know your decisions

  1. Get a baseline. Play a practice game normally (don't play extra safe), and afterwards note your good and bad decisions with the context: "upwind blade, forehand side".
  2. Decide what good means for you. A newer player might rightly avoid hard throws; a fast-flow team might count looking off an open pass as bad.
  3. Find the patterns. Pick three to five situations where bad decisions cluster, and for each one write the better option. Remember decisions aren't just throw or don't: fake, pivot, talk to cutters, get balanced, call the foul.
  4. Picture the better choice (the decision, not the execution), then repeat the exercise next game and compare.

Safe, normal, aggressive

A player who never takes risks never improves; one who takes too many is a liability. Learn what each gear feels like:

  • Safe: only safe options. Too far and you're blocked: you lose confidence even in easy throws.
  • Normal: normal and safe options.
  • Aggressive: anything sensible. Too far and you're in danger: low-percentage throws, looking off good options.

Practise a whole game in one gear and tally what you threw. Then use it on purpose: much stronger than the opponent? Play safe. Behind in a must-win game? Go aggressive.

For coaches

When drilling a skill like breaking the mark, players need permission to fail. Build in a safe option (a swing) and score it so everyone knows the order: completed break, then swing, then a turnover trying the break.

Sessions that make everyone better

Coaching throws

Watch before you fix. Most throwing problems start before the release, and old habits need drills that sneak past muscle memory.

A one-to-one throwing check

  1. Boots on. A stable base matters for every throw.
  2. Grip. Don't assume it's right just because someone's played a year. For a flick: index and middle fingers inside the rim, the rest outside. Only change it if it's actually a problem.
  3. Pivot. Watch the feet. Do they step to the same place every time?
  4. The lead-up. Watch the disc's path before release: big swoops and wind-ups add randomness. A wobbly disc means not enough snap.
  5. Lengthen the throw if you can't see the problem; extra power often exaggerates it.

Drills that build snap

  • Compass pivoting: throw from every point of the compass. Backwards pivots force the wrist to work on its own.
  • Slightly higher and lower releases than normal, to carry the new snap back into a normal throw.
  • Aim at the receiver's shins to find height control again.
  • Elbow first: for flicks, the elbow leads and the wrist follows. A follow-through across the body means the elbow isn't leading. Practise fakes with a big elbow lead.
  • Grip strength (if the disc droops or slips): three sets of 20 disc curls, grip changes, fakes and shakes, daily. It improves within a couple of weeks.

Shapes and touch

Learn three edges: outside-in, inside-out, and a rising edge for touch. Touch slows the disc so it hangs in the receiver's window; an inside-out or around break with touch is far easier to catch. A good routine: inside-out forehand fast, then soft; outside-in fast, then soft; the same four on the backhand; take three steps back and repeat.

The around backhand

  1. Wrist-only backhands from 5–10m, arm straight out.
  2. Change the release angle with the wrist while the arm stays put.
  3. From the flick stance, move the disc (not your body) to the backhand side, low, legs bent, and release in one motion.
  4. Push wider (pivot round a pole) and practise against a mark. If it's covered, go back to the flick side: that moves the mark and reopens the around.

Tips

  • Film throws from the side and front in slow motion, and compare with players you admire. Change one thing at a time.
  • Practise half pivots in a mirror. A half pivot should look exactly like the start of a full one; that's what makes a shimmy believable.
  • Coaches: try learning your off-hand. Starting from scratch again shows you what beginners feel.
  • On your own: throw at goalposts, or have someone on a call shout out the throw to make.

Sessions that make everyone better

Fast break

Right after a turnover the other team is disorganised. Know your target before you pick up the disc, spread out, and keep away from it.

Why it works

They were just set up to attack the other way, nobody's sure who's marking whom, and their stack has left big spaces. That gives you an overload (more of us than them downfield, common after a zone block) or an isolation (someone free in space, often the deepest player).

Three principles

  • Identify a target before you pick it up: forward, back or, most often, sideways. Picking it up with no plan isn't a fast break.
  • Spread the field. Cutters push away from the disc into dangerous space, and point to it. Get into the endzone and call for it.
  • Clear the dead space within about 3m of the disc. Crowding it kills the flow.

Fast isn't always immediate. With no mark and cutters spread, it's fine to take a look, or to give the disc to your best thrower and isolate your best receiver. Let players solve it in the way that suits their strengths.

A game for it

5v5 on small fields. The coach blows a whistle to create a turnover; players put the disc down. The nearest player must pick it up and the new offence fast breaks. Watch for rushing to pick up without a target, and half-speed pick-ups.

Communication turns seven people into a team. Agree how you'll talk before you need to.

Questions every team should answer

Win the moment

A short memory and being where your feet are. Your body has moved on from the drop; make sure your mind has too.

After a mistake we time-travel: back to "why did I do that?" or forward to "they'll take me off". Meanwhile the play has moved on without us. The aim isn't a perfect game. It's winning the next moment, even after losing the last ten.

1. Become a noticer

Nerves and focus

Nerves are normal. They only become a problem when they make you hesitate, overthink or tense up.

What nerves do

Sweaty hands, a racing heart, doubts, fear of messing up. They can make players freeze, rush or tire faster.

Tools that help

  • Breathing: in slowly through the nose, out slowly through the mouth.
  • Tense and release: squeeze each muscle group, then let it go.
  • A tiny goal for the next point: "keep my body position on D". It gives your attention somewhere to go.
  • Self-talk chosen in advance.
  • Mindfulness: notice thoughts and feelings without judging them, then bring attention back to the game.

Wide and narrow focus

Ultimate needs both: wide to read the whole field, narrow to execute a throw. Good players switch between them. Practise with simple exercises (count passes in a game, or count back from 100 in threes), take short breaks to reset, and build a pre-game routine that gets you in the right frame of mind.

The mental game

Getting out of your head

You don't have to get rid of nerves or negative thoughts to play well. You have to stop fighting them.

Think of it as flexibility for the mind. The goal isn't to remove the hard stuff (it turns up when it matters most). It's to play the way you want to, even while it's there.

Make room for it

Let uncomfortable thoughts and feelings come and go instead of wrestling them. Avoiding them leads to rushed warm-ups, hiding from the ball and faked injuries.

Be here

Attention on this point, not the last drop or the next round.

Know what you value

When you've played your best, what were you being? Creative? Fearless? Relentless on D?

Act on it

If you value courage, take the throws you're learning, even if you might miss.

Drop the labels

"I'm a choker" becomes "I'm having the thought that I'm a choker". You notice the story instead of living it.

Unhook from thoughts

Say "milk" over and over until it's just a sound. Thoughts like "I can't beat this player" are words too, not facts.

The mental game

Preparing for pressure

Resilient teams aren't just tough in the moment. They plan for trouble, have routines to fall back on, and learn afterwards.

Minimise: plan ahead

With the leaders (or the whole team), ask what has put pressure on us before, what's coming up, and what has worked. Then play "what if…?":

  • If a handler gets injured, who steps in?
  • Who can sub on mid-point?
  • Do we always call a timeout after three goals against in a row?
  • Do we change the O line after it's broken twice?

Manage: in the moment

  • Fix problems fast: if they're poaching our pull play, change the call now.
  • Let people speak: players can talk to leaders on the sideline, and we ask for input at half-time.
  • Stick to the plan: keep sideline talk, line-calling and trusted plays consistent, and keep players in their roles.
  • Listen to experience, not rank. The players who've been there before often know best.
  • Be honest: it's fine to say something isn't working.

Mend: learn afterwards

  • Check in with key players and anyone who found it tough.
  • A five-minute huddle after the game: what went well, what we'll change.
  • Thank the people who helped the team through it.

Tell the whole team there's a plan. Just knowing the team prepares for big moments helps people cope with them.

Planning the season

Start from the key dates, count the sessions, and finish teaching new things early enough to practise playing together.

  1. Mark the key events and count the sessions before each one.
  2. List what needs learning: resets, initiating movement, pull plays, the main offences, endzone, flow, defensive principles, zones, plus time for the team to gel and agree its calls.
  3. Finish new material early. Leave the last few full-squad sessions before the big event for playing, line rotations and new teammates learning each other's throws.

  5. One thing for about a month, then the next. Aim for two looks on offence and three or four on defence by the end.

Running a season

Squad size and playing time

Bigger squads mean fresher legs, fewer injury problems and better training. The price is less playing time, so set expectations long before it matters.

Why go bigger

  • Tiredness loses tournaments: it slows legs, decisions and emotions.
  • Fresher players get injured less, and each injury hurts less.
  • Better sessions: enough people even when life gets in the way.
  • Room for individual needs: rest an injury, miss a weekend.
  • More players with big-event experience next year.
  • More "optional efforts": chasing lost causes because you'll get a rest.

The costs

  • The average level drops.
  • Less playing time, and grumbles about it.
  • Same cost per player, fewer points played.
  • Less on-field development each.
  • Some players struggle to switch on after long rests.
  • Lines take more planning.

Making it work

  • Say exactly what people are signing up for, before trials, including how playing time works in knockout games. Complaints at Nationals are caused by unclear expectations months earlier.
  • Train for intensity, not volume. Fewer, harder reps with proper rest, so players learn that playing less but harder is more effective.
  • Have a line system for the whole season, including what changes in knockouts, when up or down five, or when key players are injured.
  • Look after everyone. In a big group it's easy for someone to drift; make sure everyone feels part of it.

One club's approach over three seasons

An Australian club took 26 players in a development year: two even squads alternating every two points, equal time all season, and "effort isn't optional" as a standard. The next year, chasing a title, it promised equal time until knockout games, when some players might not play at all. That was said clearly from the start, so it held, but watching friends sit out hurt. The year after, it promised everyone would play in every game, even if only a point or two, and won the title.

Fair trials

Trials should find the right players, show everyone what to work on, and spot who's coachable. Fairness takes design.

Designing the day

  • Use drills nobody knows. Familiar drills favour people who've done them before, and new ones show who learns quickly.
  • Drills first, then games. Drills make people show every skill, not just their favourite ones; scrimmages later let them play freely.
  • One grader, one attribute. Each assessor scores only one thing (throwing, cutting or defence), so everyone's measured the same way and nobody grades a clubmate on everything.
  • Set the scene: "imagine it's a big tournament game".

Deciding

  • Average the scores and rank, then discuss alongside the selectors' notes. Data plus judgement beats either alone.
  • Expect surprises: quiet players who do the little things right show up in the scores.
  • Some things, like competitiveness, still need judgement. And someone can just have a bad day.

Using the results

  • Group scores into themes (throwing, defence, cutting) to see the squad's biggest gaps and plan the season around them.
  • Give every player specific things to work on.
  • Fitness tests are only worth it if you'll use them: for feedback, tracking progress or selection.

What drills can't see

Mental strength, and the emotion big games need. Drills can also penalise smart players who avoid risky situations in games. Don't rely on one day.

Tactics are tools

No tactic is best in general. The best one fits your players, your goals, the opponent, the weather and how your team is feeling.

Tactics coordinate what the team does: which spaces we attack, how we like to attack, what we take away on defence, and how we react. A stack is just a shape that sets up spaces. Copying a famous team's set rarely works without their players.

A good tactic

  • Fits the players you have. Players make tactics work, not the other way round.
  • Expresses how you want to play right now: a deep game in a downwind final, quick resets in a heatwave. Changing shape can help shift the team's mindset.
  • Doesn't need to be clever or secret. Surprise is worth a point at most.

For a club like ours

Development teams should choose tactics that help players enjoy the game and grow, not just win today. Don't teach players there's only one right way to play. Aim for flexibility (being able to change how we play) and awareness (knowing when to).

Try this

  • Write the team's approach for a few situations (downwind, against a zone, protecting a lead) without mentioning any positions or shapes. If you can't, the plan isn't clear yet.

Timeouts

A timeout freezes the game. Its best use is keeping the disc. Save the chats for the sideline.

Good uses

  • To prevent a turnover. High stall, the dump's slipped, a surprise poach: call it and reset into your shape. Expect the defence to throw something new at you straight after.
  • When the offence has turned into a mess. Sixty seconds to clear space and reorganise.
  • After being broken twice in a row. Change something decisively. If a timeout on that point didn't work, change the line instead.
  • To teach when the team can't see what the opponent is doing. One voice, a whiteboard, three adjustments at most and only one in detail: "reset on stall four".
  • For rest, rarely, when short of subs. Better still, use the whole squad earlier in the day.

Usually a waste

  • Disc stuck on the sideline after a turnover: get it in quickly before the trap sets.
  • Just outside the endzone: you lose flow and give the defence a set field. Try your normal offence first.
  • Alone deep after a huck: the defence is panicking, so don't give them time.
  • No real reason, like one turfed throw. It can knock confidence.
  • Pep talks: do them between games or in small groups on the sideline.

Practise calling timeouts in scrimmages so the team knows what to do.

Safe play

Contact injuries are almost always preventable. With no referees, keeping the game safe is down to players, captains and coaches.

Why it goes wrong

Rarely malice. More often: a culture that praises being physical, highlight reels from leagues that allow more contact, and the fact that almost nobody trains how to bid safely. In ultimate's rules, every player must try to avoid contact.

What we do

  1. Assume it could happen to us, and act before it does.
  2. Win the space, not just the disc. You need room to run, bid and land without hitting someone who already has the position. No space, no play.
  3. Match the level. Give newer players a bigger margin: elite-level split-second pull-outs scare beginners and catch them out.
  4. Praise the clean pull-out, ours or the opponent's, out loud and later in team messages.
  5. Train it. Set up contest drills slowly with one rule (no contact), then build to full speed, keeping the rule.
  6. When it goes wrong, act. Look after the injured player first and apologise. Captains deal with their own player, which may mean benching them. If an opponent's play is dangerous, talk captain to captain early, and use a spirit timeout without hesitation.

  • Enjoy the people. On final morning they cooked, sang and danced together, and played feeling weightless.

A balancing act

Playing and leading at the same time is hard at every level. You'll give up a little of both, but you can keep it small.

Ask yourself: after a tournament, did leading hold back your playing, or did playing hold back your leading? Decide long term where you want to sit, from fully player to fully coach, and set yourself goals for both, just as players set development goals.

Mantra: work hard, get better, have fun. It's another reason the best player doesn't have to be captain.

Release

  • Hand off on-field jobs, like marking their best player, so you can think about tactics.
  • Ask the club for help with admin. It shows humility and gives others responsibility.
  • Ask the team to bring the energy when you can't.
  • Call lines from people who are ready at the line. Want to play? Be there and be excited.

Separate

  • Plan practices ahead, so in a drill you can be in the drill.
  • Plan substitutions ahead. Players know when they'll rest, and you choose from fewer people in the moment.
  • Use a cue for "coach mode", like a whistle instead of shouting for a huddle.
  • Set aside separate time for your own game and for leading.

Leverage

  • As a coach, selection and planning show you where the team has plenty of a skill and where there are gaps.
  • As a player, you feel the flow of a game better on the pitch than from the sideline, like knowing one more rep will get the turn.

Make it, break it

How programmes get built: notice what's working, keep it, and don't be afraid to break it to build something better.

The basic formula

Build a strong team tradition. Have fun. Compete. Rinse and repeat. Fun builds bonds; competition should spread through the team like an infection. Together they build something players want to be part of.

The advanced formula

  • Build your infrastructure.
  • Be ready for failure. Programmes don't grow overnight, and early failures can be used to motivate.

  • Build it back better, with communication and trust.

An army of dump defenders

Teach a team one job at a time. A single strategic anchor gives everyone the same goal and a solid foundation.

An exercise from the talk: in threes, take one minute to explain your job. Could anyone now do it? We do the same to new players, listing everything they need to be good at all at once. It overwhelms players and coaches alike.

The example: dump defence

Be close and present, take away the space we've agreed as a team, and challenge their other options.

Making the anchor stick

  • High volume: practise it constantly so the team keeps checking in on it.
  • Variety: the same drill every week and people switch off. Put the dump in front of other skills, like hucking off a dump pass.
  • Vocabulary: a short call like "close the door" creates a picture people recall instantly.
  • Illuminate: see the whole game through that lens.
  • Evaluate: "did we play well?" is hard to answer. "Did we shut down the dump?" is easy, and it gives players a clear sense of success.

Outliers, and moving on

Some players will need extra help with specifics, or will be more useful elsewhere (covering deep, say). Don't try to build the whole house at once: lay the foundation, and when most of the team has mastered it, add the next anchor.

From rookie to baller

The stages of a player's journey, and what moves them to the next one. Skills develop at different speeds: you can be a starter-level thrower and a rookie-level cutter.

Rookie

Athletic and keen, but not yet smart on the field: unsure of the rules, shaky throws, can't yet tell an open cutter from a guarded one. We all go through this.

Next: play more, build situational awareness, learn the rules.

Substitute

Can do one or two things very well, like big skies or long flicks, but gets in teammates' way and struggles to improvise beyond set plays.

Next: 70–80% consistency on throws, cuts, catches and positioning; awareness becomes pattern recognition; start making plays.

Starter

Reliable. Always works hard and does their job, sees plays coming, does three or four skills very well and copes with the unexpected.

Next: play on instinct; 85–95% consistency; create an advantage every time, on the mark and downfield.

Baller

The level for a Nationals team. Does almost everything very well, makes big plays from small windows, and opponents game-plan around them.

Next: plays only they can make; make teammates' jobs easier; stay at that level for a weekend, a season, several years.

G.O.A.T.

Professional-level athletic, disc and mental skills; big plays in big moments with no margin for error; guarded by the other team's best every point.

Breaking the mark

You're taught early that the mark stops you throwing to one side. It doesn't. If a teammate is open and the throw gets us closer to scoring, throw it.

The goal isn't keeping possession. It's moving towards a goal. A flat break throw gives the team its best chance (and stays flat in the wind).

Three keys

  • Manipulate the mark. A real open-side fake opens space on the break side. Or just look ready: a wide forehand stance forces the mark to respect it. Or beat them with quickness: a half-pivot release past their shoulder before they react (quick, not long).
  • Release points. High and low, and wide from your pivot. Wide releases make fakes believable and throws around the mark easier.
  • Pivot well. Not straight forward or back; directly left or right gives the best channel to a receiver behind the force.

Home position

Both hands on the disc, lined up with your centre. From there: pivot forehand, pivot backhand, half-fake forehand then backhand, the reverse, lefty, and half-pivot throws. A half fake should look exactly like a full pivot.

How to practise

  • Focus throwing: start every throw ready to throw; show the open side, then pivot to the break.
  • On your own: from home position, run through every move.
  • Wide releases: pivot, hold, throw. Throw around a tree, moving further away to force a wider pivot.

It only takes one great move to break a mark, and it's learnable. Once you have it, build its counter.

Developing great defenders

Athleticism helps, but at the top there are great defenders who aren't great athletes, and great athletes who can't defend. The difference is decisions.

When teams want a safe seventh defender, they pick speed, consistency and few obvious errors. That's a short-term fix. Defensive leaders do more: they see and read the field, are aggressive, understand risk, take intelligent gambles, decide quickly and independently, and command the game. You can coach all of this without being able to do it yourself.

Five ways to teach it

  1. Drill decisions. 1v1 builds the base; then go creative: 3v2 with the offence limited to two throws to score; last-back poach practice, where the deepest defender decides how to cover cutters going deep. Isolate a decision point and rebuild it as a drill.
  2. Drill working together. Spell out read-and-react: "if you see this, do that, because…"
  3. Bust the offence myth. Offence may have the advantage, but the real contest is our D against their O versus their D against our O.
  4. Measure athleticism in context. Use game-like tests (5-5-5-20m rather than a 40-yard dash) and let players assess themselves.
  5. Judge against realistic alternatives. Did your choice lower their chance of scoring? Then it was good, even if they scored. Containing a great cutter in a smaller space is a win.

None of this is short-term. Players have to make mistakes to learn, and mind and body develop together. That development is coaching.

Elite defence: the mental shift

Defenders start at a disadvantage: the offence moves first. So stop hunting highlights and start prioritising. The highlights will find you.

Two fundamentals

  • Take away the offender's first option: what they actually do after reading you, not what the playbook says.
  • Make them use their second and third options. Defence is mitigation, and you have to adapt.

The target

Stop option 1 about 95% of the time, option 2 about 50% and option 3 about 20%. Making a cutter do what they don't want changes how they play the whole point. Over a game, fatigue and frustration set in and errors follow.

Read before the point

Running down on the pull: where are they setting up, is my player handling or cutting, how tall are they, what's the wind doing? Try to predict your cutter's first move in every drill and every pickup game.

Getting better

In practice, attack your weaknesses; getting beaten in training doesn't matter, and the aim is to turn a weakness into a strength by game time. In games, use your strengths and drag your opponent into your comfort zone.

Don't throw it to stone hands

Catching is a learnable skill. Sports-vision drills push catchers out of their comfort zone so the game feels easy.

  • Strobe catching: throw with a partner while blinking. Add movement towards the disc, then cuts.
  • No blink: blink at release, then watch the disc, and specifically the rim, all the way in. Add movement, then cuts.
  • Lay down: the receiver lies on the ground for a gentle throw to the hands. Add a roll, or call a direction to turn.
  • Stand and turn: the receiver faces away; the thrower shouts "turn" and throws. Add movement and a called direction.
  • Awareness catching: two "defenders" wave near the receiver without blocking. Add a cut, and do twenty for conditioning.
  • Compass catching: the thrower walks round the receiver throwing from each direction; right hand only, then left, then moving.
  • Partner lean: for layout catches. One partner holds the catcher's ankles as they lean; start with close throws and lead them further out.
  • Mini-disc catching: a smaller target and wild flights. Great in warm-ups: two hands, left, right, on your toes, on cuts.

Training like the pros

Don't design a programme from scratch. Take a good one and adapt it to ultimate's movements.

The talk used the Competitive Edge Sports philosophy, MORR:

  • Movement: everything is movement, so be precise. Ladders and footwork must be sport-specific, and extra work should never spoil your mechanics.
  • Overspeed (with deceleration): assisted running faster than you can manage alone, like a resistance harness pulled out for an assisted start, to teach your body it can go faster. Then sprint to a cone and stop by the next to train braking.
  • Resistance: bands between the ankles for shuffles, bounds and pivots (take them off and feel the difference); a punch belt for marking and reaching catches.
  • Reaction: a trainable skill, with about 15% improvement for the average athlete. Shuffle one way, and on a sound and visual cue, shuffle back.

Be specific and bold: to sprint faster, sprint; to jump higher, jump. Sprint intervals are the best endurance work.
