# Captains' and coaches' guide

For the 1s and 2s captains and the club's guest coaches. It covers three things: how to captain and lead, how to think tactically about the season and about games, and how to talk to players so sessions feel good and people improve. It draws mainly on the Rise Up conference talks (Amsterdam), the Flik Deep Dives and Team Analysis, and the BFD manual, plus Flik's Coach Corner and Case Studies and the other coaching resources. Read it alongside the Leadership section of the Season plan.

**The short version**

1. **Love the team, not necessarily everyone in it.** Play hard rather than perfectly, find answers together, and make decisions you can live with (Rise Up, Leadership without an Ego).
2. **Share the load.** Captains, guest coaches and player leaders each own part of the job (Flik, Shared Leadership; BFD).
3. **One job at a time.** Pick one "strategic anchor" per block, teach it constantly, and judge games by it (Rise Up, Army of Dump Defenders).
4. **Tactics are tools.** Choose them to fit our players, the opponent and the conditions; stay flexible (Ian French, Getting Tactics Right).
5. **Short, specific, positive.** One concept, 30 seconds, then back to playing. Ask before you tell, and celebrate loudly when players do it in a game (BFD; Ian French; Flik, Effective Feedback).

## 1. How to captain and lead

### Who does what

BFD splits leadership into clear roles so no one person carries everything:

- **Coach:** runs sessions and games, always keeping the final goal and the milestones in mind. Sessions follow a technical and tactical plan so everyone shares the same plays, values and techniques.
- **Captains:** BFD suggests three, splitting offence, defence and overall team management. Their main job is to keep the group united and remind players of the objectives, values and motivations. They take team issues to the coach, represent the team to other teams, and make sure behaviour matches the sport's values and rules.
- **Managers:** a kit manager and a communications manager (schedules, event details, social media), so the captains can focus on players.

At Herd, the 1s and 2s each have their own captains with an equal voice; guest coaches and the managers sit alongside them (Season plan, Leadership).

### Share leadership

Teams that share leadership "horizontally" are more committed, more confident and perform better. Coaches who share leadership are seen as *better* leaders, not weaker ones (Flik, Shared Leadership).

- Player leaders are usually people others already like and trust, who lift the team when it's behind.
- Involve players in choosing leaders; coaches are often poor at spotting them.
- Roles can be informal. Giving a senior player a job (running the sideline, leading warm-ups, owning the stats) is a good way to use their experience.
- Make clear what each role is responsible for and why you're sharing it.

### Leadership without an ego

From the Rise Up talk of the same name:

| Myth | Fact |
| --- | --- |
| A great leader has to play well | A great leader has to play **hard**: energy and intensity lift the team more than your own stats |
| A great leader must know the answer | A great leader needs to **find** an answer, and can say "I don't know, let's work it out" |
| A great leader has to make the right decision | A great leader needs to make **a** decision and work with the outcome. Line calls can never be perfect, and the team has to accept that |
| A great leader needs to love everyone on the team | A great leader needs to love **the team**. Shared values (Revolver's are intensity, humility and discipline) let you deal with someone who's working against them |

Johnny Bravo's coach quotes a Buddhist monk on this: you need just enough ego "so that you don't step in front of a bus" (Flik Deep Dives, Johnny Bravo's Long Game).

### The four qualities of an effective leader

Leadership is a relationship with the team, not a personality type. Research suggests teams that pick captains at random actually outperform teams that elect them, because we're poor at spotting good leaders. Effective leaders embody the team's identity (Flik, Leadership):

1. **One of the team:** role-model the team's values, keep listening (especially when things are going well), chat and ask for feedback, wear the kit.
2. **A champion:** make individual success the team's success, for example "Alex's layout block came from everyone working hard on defence". Don't make success about you. Be fair, and let players set the behaviour standards at the start of the season.
3. **A storyteller:** tell the team who we are and where we're going, and what makes Herd special.
4. **A builder:** make what we actually do match our identity. For a mixed club that means drills, tactics, pullers, leadership roles and language that involve everyone equally.

**Where to start:** watch and listen first, write down what you think the team's values are, then ask players and be ready to hear something different. The Herdlodge values workshop (week 29) does this formally.

### Playing and leading at the same time

Playing and captaining together lowers your ceiling at both, but you can limit the cost (Rise Up, A Balance Act):

- **Release:**
  - Hand off on-field jobs, like marking their best player, so you can think about tactics.
  - Ask for help with admin.
  - Ask teammates to bring the energy when you can't.
- **Separate:**
  - Plan practices and substitutions in advance, so lines are mostly set and you choose from fewer players in the moment.
  - Use a cue, such as a whistle, to switch between "player" and "coach" mode.
  - Keep separate time for your own game and for leading.
- **Leverage:** you feel the flow of a game better on the pitch than from the sideline, and selecting the squad shows you which skills the team is short of.

At tournaments, a guest coach or a non-playing captain on the sideline takes some of this load off the playing captains (Plan).

### Building the programme over years

- **The basic formula:** have fun, compete, repeat. Competition should spread through the team, and bonds come from doing both together (Rise Up, Make it Break it).
- **The advanced formula:**
  - Build the club's infrastructure, and expect failure along the way.
  - Use early failures to motivate.
  - It's fine to break what works to build something better.
  - Build communication and trust. Doublewide listened to young players who challenged selection and set up an advisory board (Rise Up, Make it Break it).
- **Don't go too strict too fast.** Mooncatchers' first push for a competitive culture lost buy-in. They rebuilt with a leadership group and a multi-year plan and won EUCF 2024 (Flik Case Studies, Building a Successful Program).
- **Play your role, and have each other's backs.** PoNY 2018: everyone gave up part of their game for the team. When a star turned it over three times in the semi, the team backed him and he played a great final (Flik Case Studies, PoNY 2018).
- **Adversity is part of it.** Johnny Bravo had a player walk off mid-game at Nationals; a veteran talked him back and they won. "I wouldn't trade those bruises" (Flik Case Studies, Johnny Bravo 2022).

### Team identity: everybody in

The Rise Up talk *Everybody In* treats identity like marketing:

1. **Hone the identity:** who are we, who do we want to be, what's our mantra?
2. **Show the team's value:** a culture that matters to the players, told through kit, social media, player profiles and highlight clips. "If there's no information about your team out there, you don't matter."
3. **Live it:** cheers, team games, a bit of absurdity, and outreach such as coaching at clinics and wearing the kit.

This feeds recruitment for Phase 1 and the Herdlodge values work.

## 2. Thinking tactically about the season

### Tactics are tools

Ian French (Ranelagh and Ireland) calls himself a "tactical agnostic": no system is best in itself (Flik, Getting Tactics Right).

- **Fit the players you have.** "Players make tactics work, tactics don't make players work." Revolver's side stack works because of who Revolver have.
- **Fit the situation:**
  - The opponent, the conditions, the team's goals and the players' state of mind.
  - Ireland 2019 switched to their deep game late in the tournament: their best thrower was on fire, the wind was straight upwind-downwind, and it was hot, so quick scores mattered.
- **Shapes are not tactics.** A shape (vert, ho, side stack) only helps express an approach. Switching shape can change the team's mindset or bring in players who suit the plan.
- **Exercise:** write our approach for each situation without naming any shape or position.
- **Flexibility and awareness:** train several options and learn to recognise when to switch. If the theoretically best option isn't the one the players believe in, go with the one they're confident in.
- **Don't stand still.** Opponents adapt, so we must too.

### One strategic anchor at a time

Teaching the whole game at once overwhelms new and experienced players alike (Rise Up, Army of Dump Defenders).

- **Pick one team job:** for example, dump defence. Get physically close, take away the space we've agreed, then challenge their other options.
- **Teach it with:**
  - High volume.
  - Variety, so players don't switch off. For example, use the dump as the start of a hucking drill.
  - Vocabulary, like "close the door".
  - Illumination: see the whole game through that lens.
- **Judge games by it.** "Did we play well?" is hard to answer; "did we defend the dump?" is easy, and it gives players a clear way to feel they succeeded.
- **Move on when most players have it.** Then add the next anchor; some players will need extra individual work.

**Suggested Herd anchors (Plan):**

| Phase | Anchor |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Resets: the dump and swing |
| 2 | Breaking the mark |
| 3 | Team defence: poach and clog near the disc |

### Planning the season

- **Finish new content early.** Leave whole squad sessions just for gameplay and rotations before the big events. Leave some sessions unplanned for recaps (Flik, Planning a Season). That's why Phase 5 has no new material.
- **Focus on one thing for about a month.** Aim for two offensive looks and three or four defensive ones. Practise communication, defensive priorities and game reps every session (Flik, Planning a Season).
- **Goals:**
  - Fury set goals about improving specific parts of their game rather than winning, and treat improvement as a multi-season project (Flik Case Studies, Fury 2018).
  - BFD sets objective and subjective goals with the staff, then shares them openly with the whole squad at a pre-season meeting.
- **Use early events as tests.** Early and mid-season tournaments are checkpoints. Test game plans in practice games, sometimes a whole day on just two themes (BFD, Game plans).

### Building a game plan (BFD)

Before each event, the captains work through:

1. **Our team:** physical and technical strengths, what we've trained, our energy and motivation.
2. **The opponent:**
   - Their dangerous players and weaknesses.
   - The set plays they start with, their system and endzone plays.
   - How likely they are to break us.
3. **Context:** test match, pool or elimination game, and past results.
4. **Conditions:** surface, slope, wind and rain, start time, and back-to-back games.

Then write Plans A, B and C. Show the team only short highlights of opponent footage, and put key players and set plays on one slide or scouting card.

### Managing the squad and playing time

- In a tight 15-14 game, BFD found the top 10 players play 60-70% of points and the bottom of the roster 0-3. Plan who plays when across the game, day and tournament, and keep stats on points played.
- **Fatigue loses games.** Belgium kept sending the same tired players out in their WUC 2024 quarter-final against Japan, and their resets and decisions fell apart. A deeper rotation might have made the difference (Flik Deep Dives, Belgium v Japan QF).
- **Score efficiently to save legs.** Italy Mixed and Rhino scored with little work: front-cone goals and isolations. That pays off late in a long tournament (Flik Deep Dives, Italy Mixed WUC 2024; Rhino 2024).

### Judging decisions

- **Judge against the realistic alternative.** Ask whether a decision made it more or less likely they'd score. Containing a great cutter in a smaller space is a win (Rise Up, Developing Great Defenders).
- **Beware false positives.** Johnny Bravo refuse to praise a low-percentage play just because it worked, and don't abandon a good plan because it failed once (Flik Case Studies, Johnny Bravo 2022).
- **Defence is mitigation.** Elite defenders don't hunt highlights. They aim to stop the offence's first option 95% of the time, the second 50% and the third 20%, so fatigue and frustration build (Rise Up, Elite Defense Mental Shift).

## 3. Talking to players: coaching for good vibes

### What players need

Players stay motivated when three needs are met: **autonomy** (some control), **competence** (feeling capable) and **relatedness** (feeling connected). Autonomy matters most (Flik, Motivating Players).

- **Involve players in decisions:** tactics, team goals, their own targets.
- **Give real choice in drills.** In a cutting drill, don't put out cones for where to cut; let players choose how to get free.
- **Explain why** when there's no choice, for example why a footwork drill helps them get open.
- **Make mistakes safe.** Say which mistakes are fine in this drill, for example dropping a disc you attacked properly. Encourage the player who makes them.
- **Keep ranking to about 30% of training.** The rest is about each player and the team improving.

### Ask, then tell

- **Questions over instructions:** "What did you notice?", "What would you do differently?" Players who find the solution themselves recognise it in games (Ian French, Games for Coaching).
- **No more than three key points per topic.** Tell, show (a whiteboard, or a demo at half speed), then do (Flik, Coaching Fundamentals).
- **One focus per session**, announced at the start, and reminded before the final game (BFD).
- **During play, stop for 30 seconds and give one concept.** Words get in the way of muscle memory (BFD).

### How to give feedback

From Flik's *Effective Feedback* (The Art of Coaching Ultimate):

1. **Start with a real positive:** "Great wrist snaps, and I love that everyone's stepping out."
2. **Make the correction by asking:** "Why would I step out like this rather than this?"
3. **Show something they can copy,** then let them practise while you walk round.
4. **Encourage the change:** "I want to see those step-outs for the next two minutes."
5. **When they do it in a game, lose your mind.** Nothing locks in learning like seeing the coach get excited.

DK suggests a "feedback sandwich" (positive, fix, positive), but Coyle found that the best cultures separate the two: big, clear bursts of praise on their own, and corrections as a two-way conversation that starts by asking if the player wants feedback. Use the four steps above for quick technical fixes in drills, and keep bigger feedback for a one-to-one. Experienced players should give feedback between points too (DK; The Culture Code).

**Be specific.** "Nice" teaches nothing; "great job cutting early when the disc reached the sideline" does. Link praise to the team's values (Flik, Leadership).

### Mistakes, nerves and belief

- **Freedom to fail.** Show what success looks like and point to examples where the team did the right thing, even in lost points (Flik Case Studies, Johnny Bravo 2022).
- **No "sorry" at training.** Nobody is trying to make mistakes (Flik Case Studies, Ranelagh 2022).
- **Positive but honest.** Don't pretend a loss doesn't hurt; turn the energy towards what's next. Reframe nerves: "I'm not nervous, I'm excited" (Flik Case Studies, Ring 2021).
- **Have their back.** Trust that everyone is trying to play their role (Flik Case Studies, PoNY 2018).
- **Plan for setbacks.** Ireland 2019 built resilience by facing setbacks all season, so losing a pool game to a team didn't shake their belief in the rematch (Flik Case Studies, Ireland 2019).
- **Confidence comes from reps.** Expose players to the situations they fear, such as being trapped in a cup, until they're familiar. Add self-talk and breathing (Flik, Self Confidence).
- **Players develop at different speeds,** and unevenly across skills: someone can be a "starter" thrower and a "rookie" cutter at once. Set expectations by stage (Rise Up, From Rookie to Baller).

### Relationships

Better relationships mean more motivated players who perform better (Flik, Building Relationships with Players):

- Be proactive; don't wait for problems. Make time away from training, and listen to understand their view.
- Keep showing you believe in them, especially in skills they can't do yet.
- Don't rush to fix things. Let a frustrated player be frustrated, then ask how you can help.
- Deal with problems quickly, because worrying about you distracts them.
- Look after yourself too.

### On the sideline and at tournaments

- **Sideline buddies:** pair players by line or role so the sideline gives useful, specific help, and so mistakes get talked through between teammates (BFD).
- **Give information, not orders:** "Your player's probably cutting under" rather than "stop the under!" (Ben Wiggins, Handler O and D).
- **Make sideline talk a process goal,** for example three useful calls per point to a player on the pitch (Rise Up, Process over Outcome).
- **Timeouts:** one message, one play (Flik, Time Outs).
- **Line calls:** prioritise players who are ready at the line (Rise Up, A Balance Act). Plan rotations in advance so nobody guesses whether they're playing.

### Phrasebook (Plan)

| Situation | Try saying | Instead of |
| --- | --- | --- |
| After a drop in a drill | "Good attack on it. Same again." | Silence, or "come on!" |
| Correcting technique | "What do you think stopped that break throw?" | "No, that's wrong." |
| Praising | "That early cut from the sideline was exactly the plan." | "Nice." |
| Before a big game | "Three things we control: talk, early cuts, back each other up." | "We have to win this." |
| After a lost game | "That hurt. Here's what we did well, and here's what we'll fix on Thursday." | Silence, or blaming |
| Between points | "Your player loves the open-side under; take it away." | "Stop getting beaten!" |
| A player on the bench a lot | (In private, before the game) "Here's your role today and when you'll be on." | Leaving them guessing |
| Someone frustrated | "What's bothering you? How can I help?" | Fixing it straight away |

## 4. Ideas from three books

Three books that shape how Herd is led: *The Captain Class* (Sam Walker) on who should lead, *The Culture Code* (Daniel Coyle) on how great groups feel, and *The Inner Game of Ultimate Frisbee* (Anthony Adams, after Timothy Gallwey) on the mental game.

### The Captain Class: what the greatest captains had in common

Walker studied the most dominant teams in sports history. Their winning streaks began and ended with one player's arrival or departure, and that player was usually the captain. Those captains were rarely the stars, rarely charismatic, and didn't give speeches. They shared seven traits:

1. **Doggedness:** total commitment in every game and every session. Teams slack off together unless one person visibly gives everything, and these captains kept the team competing even when a game was won.
2. **Playing to the edge:** controlled, purposeful aggression rather than hostility. *For Herd:* in a self-refereed sport this means relentless, physical-within-the-rules intensity on defence, never at the expense of Spirit (Plan).
3. **Doing the thankless jobs:** they carried the water and took the unglamorous role, earning the moral authority to push the team in tough moments. The easiest way to lead is to serve.
4. **Practical, constant communication:** not speeches, but a steady stream of chatter, circulating and talking to everyone, with gestures and touch.
5. **Wordless displays:** deliberate, emotional, non-verbal moments that lift the team when words aren't enough.
6. **The courage to stand apart:** willing to argue about *how the team plays* or to defend teammates, but never personal conflict.
7. **Emotional control:** a "kill switch" for anger and setbacks so they don't spread to the team. It can be practised.

Walker also warns against the false idols of talent, charisma, the big speech and the fiery icon. A coach does best with a captain acting as their partner on the pitch.

**At Herd:** use these seven traits, not "best player" or "loudest voice", when choosing captains and player leaders. They fit the Rise Up advice to play hard rather than well.

### The Culture Code: safety, vulnerability, purpose

Coyle found that great groups share three skills.

**1. Build safety.** People are constantly checking whether they're safe in a group. "Belonging cues" answer yes when they have **energy** (you're invested in the conversation), **individualisation** (the person feels known and valued) and **future orientation** (the relationship will continue). One "bad apple" can drag a group down, and good apples can lift it.

- **Mark threshold moments:** a new player's first session, the trials, squad announcements. Make arrival feel special.
- **Show you're listening;** admit your fallibility early ("What am I missing?"); thank the person who brings bad news; overdo thank-yous.
- **Make sure everyone has a voice.** In one-to-ones, Captain Mike Abrashoff asked every sailor three questions: what do you like most, what do you like least, and what would you change if you were in charge?
- **Feedback:** separate praise from correction rather than sandwiching them. Say you're giving the feedback because you have high standards for them and believe they can reach them.
- **Have fun.**

**2. Share vulnerability.** Cooperation is built in small moments where someone admits a weakness and others respond.

- **The leader goes first:** "I screwed that up" are the most important words a leader can say.
- **Deliver bad news in person,** and link discipline to reconnecting.
- **Listen like a trampoline:** support, ask questions that gently challenge, and add energy. Resist jumping in with your own answer.
- **After-action review (AAR),** five questions:
  1. What did we intend?
  2. What happened?
  3. Why?
  4. What will we keep?
  5. What will we change? Sometimes run it without the leaders in the room.
- **Candour, not brutal honesty;** and sometimes the leader steps back so the group runs itself.

**3. Establish purpose.**

- **Name and rank your priorities,** then be ten times clearer about them than feels necessary.
- **Proficiency or creativity:**
  - Some skills need machine-like reliability (proficiency): resets, marks, pull coverage. Teach them with clear models, high repetition, lots of feedback and "if this, then that" rules.
  - Others need creativity: offence flow and adapting game plans. Give players freedom, make it safe to fail, and celebrate hugely when they take the initiative.
- **Catchphrases:** simple, action-based, a bit corny, like the All Blacks' "leave the jersey in a better place".
- **Measure what matters,** and use artifacts that remind people what the team stands for.
- **One "bar-setting" behaviour:** Quinnipiac hockey built their identity on "Forty for Forty", full effort on all forty back-checks a game, though almost none of them pays off. A Herd version might be every pull chased at full speed, or every dump defended (Plan).

### The Inner Game of Ultimate Frisbee: the Heckler and the Hero

Gallwey noticed his tennis students improved more when he said *less* and asked them to watch themselves without judging. Adams applies this to ultimate with two selves:

- **The Heckler** (Gallwey's "Self 1") instructs, judges and doubts. It overthinks, compares, worries about the future and clings to how things "should" go.
- **The Hero** ("Self 2") is the trained body and instincts: flow, intuition, adaptability, confidence and present focus.
- The Heckler is useful for planning and review; in games, trust the Hero.

**Key themes:**

- **Awareness drives improvement:** physical, spatial, situational, emotional, mental and team awareness.
- **Relaxed concentration and effortless effort:** fully focused but loose. Use cue words like "soft hands" or "patient and sharp".
- **Pressure is a challenge, not a threat.** Use breathing, visualisation and a pre-point routine.
- **Flow** comes from the right balance of challenge and skill, clear goals and present focus, and teams can reach it together.
- **Non-verbal team synergy** can be trained, for example with scrimmages where talking is gradually removed.
- **Competing with heart:** top-level competitiveness and Spirit together.

**For coaches:** say less. Ask players to *notice* ("where did that release finish?", "what did you see before you cut?") rather than judging. This fits Ian French's questioning approach.
